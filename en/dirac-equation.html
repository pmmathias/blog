Prologue

Antimatter in the hospital

Anyone who undergoes a PET scan in a hospital is first injected with a weakly radioactive sugar solution. The sugar is labelled with the isotope fluorine-18, which is produced in a cyclotron and has a half-life of just under two hours. Inside the body, the solution accumulates wherever a great deal of sugar is consumed – in active regions of the brain, in the heart muscle, in fast-growing tumours. And there, in the patient’s tissue, something remarkable happens: when a fluorine-18 nucleus decays, it emits a positron, a particle of antimatter. On average it travels less than a millimetre, meets an electron, and both disappear. What remains are two quanta of light, each carrying 511 kiloelectronvolts of energy, which fly off in opposite directions. The scanner’s detector ring registers them simultaneously, and from millions of such pairs a computer assembles the image.

The existence of antimatter was predicted in 1931, before anyone had seen it. The prediction came not from an experiment but from an equation that Paul Dirac, then 25 years old, had written down in January 1928. He was looking for an equation for the electron that would satisfy both quantum mechanics and Einstein’s theory of relativity, and in the process he ran into a mathematical problem: he had to take a square root that cannot be taken with ordinary numbers. The solution he found fits on a single line:

\[ \big(i\hbar\,\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - mc\big)\,\psi = 0 \]

Three things followed from this line that nobody had asked for. First, the electron carries an internal angular momentum, its spin, which until then had been known only from experiment and had to be built into the theory by hand. Second, this spin has a property that seems to contradict all intuition: an electron must turn around twice before it is back in its initial state. Third, for every solution with positive energy the equation has a second one with negative energy – and Dirac, after some false starts, concluded that this second solution describes a particle with the mass of the electron but the opposite charge.

On 2 August 1932, at the California Institute of Technology, Carl Anderson photographed the track of such a particle in a cloud chamber. He knew of Dirac’s theory, but it played no part in the discovery; he had come across the track while studying cosmic rays. Dirac received the Nobel Prize in 1933, Anderson in 1936. Today the positron is put to use every day in hospitals all over the world.

This post leads to Dirac’s line without assuming a physics degree. What is needed is vectors, matrices and school mathematics. The aim is more than a retelling: every symbol of the equation is to be earned in the course of the text, which means that it should become clear why it stands where it does and why it cannot look any different. The bar along the top edge shows which symbols have already been earned.

The route has three acts, and each one answers a question.

  • Act I · Geometry. Is there a product of vectors that throws nothing away? The answer leads to oriented areas, to the imaginary unit as a rotation, and to a curious half angle.
  • Act II · Quantum mechanics. What does nature do between two measurements? The answer is that it rotates phasors and adds them up, and matrix multiplication turns out to be exactly that: the summing of all possibilities of one step.
  • Act III · Relativity. How does one take the square root of \(E^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4\)? The answer is supplied by the geometry of Act I, and from it follow spin, antimatter and a surprisingly intuitive picture of mass.

The common thread that connects the three acts is rotation.

Act I · Geometry

Multiplication can rotate

Guiding question: Is there a product of vectors that throws nothing away?

Section 1

Vectors cannot be divided

With numbers one can answer a question so self-evident that it hardly registers: by what must 3 be multiplied to obtain 6? The answer is \(6/3 = 2\), and it exists because one can divide by every number except zero. The same question can be asked of arrows. Let two vectors \(u\) and \(v\) in the plane be given. By what object must \(u\) be multiplied so that \(v\) comes out? Intuitively the answer is clear: \(u\) must be stretched to the length of \(v\) and turned into the direction of \(v\). So there is a well-defined operation that carries \(u\) into \(v\) – a rotation-scaling. So we are looking for an object \(Q\) with \(uQ = v\), and the question is whether it can be written as a quotient \(Q = u^{-1}v\), in the way one writes \(6/3\) for numbers. That it is stated explicitly on which side of \(u\) the object \(Q\) stands is no accident: it will turn out that for vectors the order of the factors matters.

School mathematics knows two products of vectors, and both fail at this question. The dot product \(u\cdot v = |u|\,|v|\cos\theta\) turns two vectors into a single number. In doing so, information is lost: infinitely many vectors \(v\) have the same dot product with \(u\), and from the number alone it is impossible to work back to which one it was. The cross product \(u \times v\) does return a vector, but it has three shortcomings at once: it exists only in three-dimensional space; it is not associative, which means that \((u\times v)\times w\) and \(u \times (v \times w)\) are in general different; and it, too, cannot be inverted, since all vectors \(v + \lambda u\) give the same cross product with \(u\).

Try it: Drag the tips of \(u\) and \(v\). Step 1 shows all vectors that have the same dot product with \(u\), step 2 all those that span the same area with \(u\). Step 3 overlays the two.

The experiment shows two things. First, each of the two measures on its own throws away exactly the information that the other one contains: the dot product measures how far \(v\) reaches in the direction of \(u\) and forgets how far \(v\) deviates sideways from it; the area measures the sideways deviation and forgets the component along \(u\). Second, the two together determine the vector \(v\) uniquely. This means that the combination of dot product and area contains everything that characterises \(v\) relative to \(u\).

This proves nothing yet, however; it only gives grounds for a suspicion. One could write the two numbers down as a pair and declare the pair to be the “product” – that would be exactly the kind of arbitrary stipulation this post sets out to avoid. The next two sections therefore proceed differently. They do not ask which product one would like to have, but which minimum requirements any product must meet if one is to calculate with it at all, and then derive what necessarily follows from these requirements. The dot product and the area are not introduced along the way. They fall out.

Section 2

Two requirements – and the dot product falls out

What we are looking for is a product \(uv\) of vectors with which one can calculate as with numbers. Exactly two requirements are placed on this product.

input First: brackets and multiplying out. The product is to be associative, \((uv)w = u(vw)\), and distributive from both sides, \(u(v+w) = uv + uw\) and \((u+v)w = uw + vw\). In addition, ordinary numbers are to take part as usual: \(\lambda u\) is the vector stretched by the factor \(\lambda\), a number may stand at any position in a product, \((\lambda u)v = \lambda\,(uv) = u\,(\lambda v)\), and numbers, vectors and products may be added, just as school lets one write \(3 + 2i\). What kind of objects such sums are, the calculation will show; nothing is assumed about them. These two rules are not a matter of taste. Without associativity one may not move a bracket in a product of three factors, without distributivity one may not multiply out a single term – and without these two moves not a single equation can be rearranged. What is not required is worth noting: that \(uv = vu\). Whether the product is commutative is left open; Section 3 will show that it cannot be.

input Second: a vector times itself gives its squared length.

\[ vv = |v|^2 \]

This is the only place where geometry enters the calculation, and it therefore deserves a justification. For numbers, \(3 \cdot 3 = 9\) is the area of a square with side length 3; squaring is the point at which multiplication and length are connected. If one demands the same of vectors, the product is tied to the measurement of lengths – otherwise it would have nothing to do with the geometry of arrows. In the interest of honesty it should be noted that one could also require \(vv = -|v|^2\); that yields a different, equally consistent algebra. This freedom of sign is not a flaw; it will play a decisive role in the third act, where physics will determine which sign applies to space and which to time.

follows necessarily These two requirements already imply the dot product. To see this, the second requirement is applied to the sum vector \(u + v\), and the product is multiplied out using the first requirement:

\[ |u+v|^2 = (u+v)(u+v) = uu + uv + vu + vv = |u|^2 + uv + vu + |v|^2 \]

Rearranging gives

\[ uv + vu = |u+v|^2 - |u|^2 - |v|^2 \]

The right-hand side is a pure number, and a familiar one. Placed tip to tail, the arrows \(u\) and \(v\) form a triangle with \(u+v\). If \(\theta\) is the angle between \(u\) and \(v\), then in this triangle the side \(u+v\) is opposite not \(\theta\) but the supplementary angle \(180^\circ - \theta\). The school law of cosines, \(c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos\gamma\), together with \(\cos(180^\circ-\theta) = -\cos\theta\), therefore gives \(|u+v|^2 = |u|^2 + |v|^2 + 2|u|\,|v|\cos\theta\). So the right-hand side equals \(2|u|\,|v|\cos\theta = 2\,u\cdot v\), and hence

\[ \tfrac12\,(uv + vu) = u \cdot v \]

Pictured intuitively, this means: \(uv\) and \(vu\) are in general different, since commutativity was not required. Their average, however, is always an ordinary number, namely the familiar dot product. It was neither defined nor introduced, but is a consequence of the requirement that squaring measures lengths. With that, however, only half of the product is understood. What remains open is what is left of \(uv\) once this average is subtracted – and that leads to an object that does not appear in school mathematics.

Section 3

The remainder is an area

follows necessarily Every product can be split into a symmetric and an antisymmetric part, just as every matrix can be split into a symmetric and an antisymmetric component:

\[ uv = \underbrace{\tfrac12(uv + vu)}_{=\;u\cdot v} \;+\; \underbrace{\tfrac12(uv - vu)}_{=:\;u\wedge v} \]

We denote the second part by \(u \wedge v\) (read “u wedge v”) and call it the wedge product. The decomposition is not a definition but an identity: it holds because the two summands add up to \(uv\). This also disposes of an obvious objection. A number and an arrow are not being added here arbitrarily; rather, an object that already exists, the product \(uv\), is being split into two components. The only question is what the second component is.

Three of its properties can be read off directly. First, \(u\wedge v\) changes sign when the factors are swapped: \(v \wedge u = -\,u\wedge v\). Second, it vanishes for parallel vectors. For if \(v = \lambda u\), then \(uv = \lambda\,uu = \lambda |u|^2 = vu\), and the antisymmetric part is zero. In particular, \(u\wedge u = 0\). Third, it does not change when \(v\) is sheared along \(u\), because by distributivity \(u\wedge(v + \lambda u) = u\wedge v + \lambda\, u\wedge u = u \wedge v\). An object that changes sign when its sides are swapped, vanishes for parallel sides and stays the same under shearing is familiar from geometry: it is the oriented area of the parallelogram spanned by \(u\) and \(v\).

follows necessarily That \(u \wedge v\) actually measures this area, and does not merely share its symmetries, can be calculated. To do so, the product \((uv)(vu)\) is computed in two ways. Using associativity and the second requirement, one obtains on the one hand

\[ (uv)(vu) = u\,(vv)\,u = |v|^2\, uu = |u|^2\,|v|^2 . \]

On the other hand, \(uv = s + B\) and \(vu = s - B\), with the abbreviations \(s = u\cdot v\) and \(B = u \wedge v\); swapping the factors leaves the first part unchanged and flips the sign of the second. Since \(s\) is an ordinary number and by the first requirement may stand at any position, \(sB = Bs\), and the two mixed terms cancel: \((s+B)(s-B) = s^2 - sB + Bs - B^2 = s^2 - B^2\). Equating the two gives

\[ B^2 = s^2 - |u|^2|v|^2 = |u|^2|v|^2\,(\cos^2\theta - 1) = -\,\big(|u|\,|v|\sin\theta\big)^2 . \]

The expression \(|u|\,|v|\sin\theta\) is exactly the area of the parallelogram formed by \(u\) and \(v\). Pictured intuitively, this means: the antisymmetric part of the product squares to minus the square of the area it spans. Something more fundamental follows from this result. No real number has a negative square, and by the second requirement no vector has one either. Provided \(u\) and \(v\) are not parallel (otherwise \(u\wedge v = 0\)), \(u\wedge v\) is therefore neither a number nor a vector but an object of its own kind. Its size is the area, as just computed. Its sense of rotation comes from the first property: swap \(u\) and \(v\), and the parallelogram is traversed the other way round while the sign flips. And it knows the plane in which \(u\) and \(v\) lie; that different planes give different area objects is shown by the coordinate calculation further below. It is called a bivector, an oriented piece of area.

The area as a calculational object in its own right was introduced by Hermann Grassmann in 1844 in his Ausdehnungslehre (“theory of extension”), written while he was a grammar-school teacher in Stettin and largely ignored by the experts of his time. In 1878, William Kingdon Clifford combined Grassmann’s areas with Hamilton’s quaternions into exactly the product derived here. He called it the “geometric product”. What differs in the presentation of this post is that the area is not introduced as a new object but recognised as what remains of \(uv\) once the dot product is subtracted.

Perpendicular means: anticommuting

follows necessarily For two perpendicular vectors the dot product is zero, and the product consists of the area alone. With the result of Section 2, this means \(uv + vu = 0\), and hence

\[ u \perp v \quad\Longrightarrow\quad uv = -\,vu . \]

Perpendicular vectors anticommute: swapping them reverses the sign. This, too, was not chosen. It also shows that the product cannot be commutative. For if \(e_1e_2 = e_2e_1\), then together with \(e_1e_2 = -e_2e_1\) it would follow that \(e_1e_2 = 0\). But then \(e_2 = (e_1e_1)\,e_2 = e_1\,(e_1e_2) = 0\) as well – a contradiction. Commutativity is therefore not something one gives up, but something that is incompatible with the two requirements.

Key point: For a basis of perpendicular unit vectors,

\[ e_1e_1 = e_2e_2 = 1, \qquad e_1e_2 = -\,e_2e_1 . \]

Normalised means: squares to 1. Perpendicular means: anticommutes. Both rules follow from the two requirements of Section 2. They are the entire grammar of this post – all the way to the Dirac equation.

In coordinates

With these two rules, the product of any two vectors of the plane can be worked out completely. For \(u = u_1e_1 + u_2e_2\) and \(v = v_1e_1+v_2e_2\), multiplying out gives four terms; two of them contain \(e_1e_1 = e_2e_2 = 1\), and in the term with \(e_2e_1\) one substitutes \(e_2e_1 = -e_1e_2\):

\[ uv = u_1v_1\,e_1e_1 + u_1v_2\,e_1e_2 + u_2v_1\,e_2e_1 + u_2v_2\,e_2e_2 = (u_1v_1 + u_2v_2) + (u_1v_2 - u_2v_1)\, e_1 e_2 . \]

The first part is the dot product in coordinates, the second is \(u\wedge v\):

\[ u\wedge v = (u_1v_2 - u_2v_1)\; e_1 e_2 . \]

The coefficient is the determinant familiar from linear algebra, the signed area of the parallelogram. Every area object of the plane is therefore a multiple of the unit area \(e_1e_2\). In space there are three basis areas, \(e_1e_2\), \(e_2e_3\) and \(e_3e_1\), one for each coordinate plane, and the same calculation gives the three coefficients \(u_1v_2 - u_2v_1\), \(u_2v_3 - u_3v_2\) and \(u_3v_1 - u_1v_3\). These are exactly the components of the cross product. The cross product is therefore an area disguised as a vector; Section 6 returns to this.

Dividing by vectors

follows necessarily The original question can now be answered. From \(vv = |v|^2\) it follows directly that \(v\) has an inverse as soon as \(v \neq 0\):

\[ v^{-1} = \frac{v}{|v|^2}, \qquad \text{since}\quad v\,\frac{v}{|v|^2} = \frac{|v|^2}{|v|^2} = 1 . \]

A concrete example makes the gain visible. Let \(u = 3e_1 + e_2\) and \(v = e_1 + 2e_2\), the starting vectors of the experiment above. Then \(u\cdot v = 5\) and \(u\wedge v = (3\cdot 2 - 1\cdot 1)\,e_1e_2 = 5\,e_1e_2\), so \(uv = 5 + 5\,e_1e_2\). With \(u^{-1} = u/10\), \(v\) can be recovered from \(u\) and the product:

\[ u^{-1}(uv) = \tfrac{1}{10}\,(3e_1 + e_2)(5 + 5\,e_1e_2) = \tfrac{1}{2}\,(3e_1 + e_2) + \tfrac12\,(3e_2 - e_1) = e_1 + 2e_2 = v . \]

(In the second summand, \(e_1e_1e_2 = e_2\) and \(e_2e_1e_2 = -e_1e_2e_2 = -e_1\) were used – each time only the two rules from the key-point box.) This means that the geometric product loses no information: from \(u\) and \(uv\), \(v\) can be reconstructed uniquely, just as the experiment in Section 1 suggested.

Finally, it is worth noting what the quotient we were looking for actually is. The object by which \(u\) must be multiplied to obtain \(v\) is \(u^{-1}v = (u\cdot v + u\wedge v)/|u|^2\) – a number plus an area. In the example it is \(\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\,e_1e_2\). By the preliminary reasoning of Section 1, this object must be a rotation-scaling. The next section shows that it is, and along the way an old acquaintance appears whose origin seemed mysterious for centuries.

Section 4

An area whose square is \(-1\)

According to the previous sections, the plane contains exactly four kinds of basic building blocks: the number \(1\), the two basis vectors \(e_1\) and \(e_2\), and the product \(e_1e_2\). Since \(e_1\) and \(e_2\) are perpendicular, their dot product is zero, and \(e_1e_2 = e_1\wedge e_2\) is a pure area: the oriented unit square, traversed from \(e_1\) to \(e_2\). Everything that can be formed in the plane by adding and multiplying is a combination of these four building blocks.

follows necessarily What does this unit square give when multiplied by itself? The result of Section 3 already supplies the answer: a bivector squares to minus the square of its area, and the area of the unit square is 1. The same answer can also be obtained without any formula, using only the two rules from the key-point box. The following puzzle does exactly that.

Try it: Simplify \((e_1e_2)(e_1e_2)\) using only two moves: two different neighbours may be swapped, which costs a minus sign. Two equal neighbours merge into their square, here \(+1\).

Written out, the calculation reads

\[ (e_1e_2)(e_1e_2) = e_1\,(e_2e_1)\,e_2 = -\,e_1\,(e_1e_2)\,e_2 = -\,(e_1e_1)(e_2e_2) = -1 . \]

A single swap, a single minus sign, and two squares that each give 1. This means: the oriented unit square of the plane squares to \(-1\). An object with this property is familiar from school, namely the imaginary unit \(i\). Sharing the same equation, however, is not yet enough to speak of “the same object”. What matters is whether \(e_1e_2\) also behaves like \(i\) – and for that one has to look at what it does to vectors.

What \(e_1e_2\) does to an arrow

follows necessarily Multiplying the two basis vectors from the right by \(e_1e_2\), the same two rules give

\[ e_1\,(e_1e_2) = (e_1e_1)\,e_2 = e_2, \qquad e_2\,(e_1e_2) = -\,e_2\,(e_2e_1) = -\,(e_2e_2)\,e_1 = -\,e_1 . \]

\(e_1\) becomes \(e_2\), and \(e_2\) becomes \(-e_1\). Pictured intuitively, this is a rotation by 90° anticlockwise, and since the product is distributive, not only every basis vector but every vector of the plane is rotated in this way. The unit square is therefore not only an area but at the same time a rotation instruction: “Rotate by a quarter turn in my plane, in my sense of rotation.”

Try it: Multiply the arrow by \(e_1e_2\) several times and watch what happens after two steps. The slider shows the rotation by an arbitrary angle.

With this, the most mysterious equation of school mathematics acquires an intuitive meaning. Rotated twice by 90°, every arrow points in the opposite direction, and “pointing in the opposite direction” is exactly multiplication by \(-1\). Pictured intuitively, the statement \(i^2 = -1\) means: two quarter turns make a half turn. After four quarter turns one is back where one started, which is why \(i^4 = 1\).

convention That the rotation runs anticlockwise is because we multiplied from the right, wrote \(e_1\) to the left of \(e_2\), and – as usual – drew \(e_2\) a quarter turn anticlockwise from \(e_1\). Multiplied from the left, \(e_1e_2\) rotates clockwise, as can be switched in the widget: \((e_1e_2)\,e_1 = -e_2\). Which side and which sense of rotation count as “positive” is an agreement that mathematics has made uniformly for centuries; it changes nothing about the geometry.

Rotation by any angle – and the quotient from Section 1

follows necessarily A rotation by an arbitrary angle \(\theta\) is obtained by multiplying the vector by a mixture of number and area:

\[ e_1\,(\cos\theta + e_1e_2\sin\theta) = \cos\theta\; e_1 + \sin\theta\; e_2 . \]

On the right stands exactly the unit vector rotated by \(\theta\) relative to \(e_1\). Likewise \(e_2\,(\cos\theta + e_1e_2\sin\theta) = \cos\theta\; e_2 - \sin\theta\; e_1\), so \(e_2\) is rotated by the same angle. Since both basis vectors are rotated by \(\theta\), distributivity makes this hold for every vector. The factor \(\cos\theta + e_1e_2\sin\theta\) is therefore the rotation instruction for the angle \(\theta\). How it is written as an exponential function, and why that makes sense, is shown in Section 7.

This finally answers the question of Section 1. In Section 3, for \(u = 3e_1 + e_2\) and \(v = e_1 + 2e_2\), the quotient came out as

\[ u^{-1}v = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12\,e_1e_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\,\big(\cos 45^\circ + e_1e_2 \sin 45^\circ\big) . \]

It rotates by 45° and scales by the factor \(1/\sqrt2\). That is exactly what is needed, because \(u\) and \(v\) enclose an angle of 45°, and \(v\), with length \(\sqrt5\), is shorter by the factor \(1/\sqrt2\) than \(u\), with length \(\sqrt{10}\). The quotient of two vectors is therefore indeed a rotation-scaling.

Complex numbers are number plus area

follows necessarily Objects of the form \(a + b\,e_1e_2\) can be added and multiplied, and their multiplication follows from \((e_1e_2)^2 = -1\) alone:

\[ (a + b\,e_1e_2)(c + d\,e_1e_2) = (ac - bd) + (ad + bc)\,e_1e_2 . \]

Symbol for symbol, this is the multiplication rule of complex numbers, with \(e_1e_2\) in place of \(i\). This is not an analogy but the same structure: complex numbers are number plus oriented area of the plane, and multiplying by them means rotating and scaling.

One distinction deserves attention here, because school mathematics usually blurs it. The vector with coordinates \((a, b)\) is \(a\,e_1 + b\,e_2\), an arrow. The complex number \(a + b\,i\) is \(a + b\,e_1e_2\), a rotation-scaling. Both have two components, and in the complex plane (the Gaussian plane) they are drawn as the same point, but they are different objects. The bridge between them is a single multiplication:

\[ e_1\,(a\,e_1 + b\,e_2) = a + b\,e_1e_2 . \]

Pictured intuitively, this means: the complex number is the rotation-scaling that carries the reference arrow \(e_1\) into the vector \((a, b)\). convention Which arrow serves as the reference is a choice – the complex plane makes it tacitly by fixing the real axis. Because this choice is so self-evident in the plane, vector and complex number seem to coincide there. In space this will no longer work, and that is precisely why space needs more than the complex numbers.

The square root of \(-1\) first appeared in 1545 in Gerolamo Cardano’s Ars Magna, as an intermediate result that Cardano himself considered meaningless. In 1637 René Descartes called such quantities “imaginaires”, imaginary, and the name has stuck. Only Caspar Wessel (1799), Jean-Robert Argand (1806) and Carl Friedrich Gauss (1831) interpreted multiplication by \(i\) as a rotation in the plane. Yet even for them, \(i\) remains a symbol whose property \(i^2 = -1\) is postulated. The route taken here reverses the order: all that was required is that squaring measures lengths. That there is an object with square \(-1\), that it is the plane itself and that it rotates – all of this follows.

Interim result: \(i\) is not an invented number but the oriented unit square of the plane. Multiplying by it means rotating by 90°. Complex numbers are number plus area – and therefore rotation-scalings.

Section 5

Three dimensions: mirrors, rotors and Hamilton’s quaternions

In space a third basis vector \(e_3\) is added, and with it three basis areas instead of one: \(e_1e_2\), \(e_2e_3\) and \(e_3e_1\). They are the oriented unit squares in the three coordinate planes. follows necessarily The same calculation as in Section 4 applies to each of them, so each squares to \(-1\). Moreover, any two of them anticommute; for instance, \(e_1e_2\,e_2e_3 = e_1e_3\), whereas \(e_2e_3\,e_1e_2 = -\,e_1e_3\). In space, therefore, there is not one but three quantities with square \(-1\), and each belongs to a plane.

Why one-sided multiplication no longer suffices in space

The obvious idea would be to treat rotations in space exactly as in the plane: to multiply a vector from the right by \(\cos\theta + B\sin\theta\), where \(B\) is the plane of rotation. follows necessarily This fails, and visibly so. Suppose \(e_1\) is to be rotated in the plane \(e_2e_3\) – a plane to which \(e_1\) is perpendicular. Then \(e_1\) ought to stay unchanged, because an arrow perpendicular to the plane of rotation is the axis of rotation. The one-sided calculation, however, gives

\[ e_1\,(\cos\theta + e_2e_3\sin\theta) = \cos\theta\; e_1 + \sin\theta\; e_1e_2e_3 . \]

The second summand is a product of three perpendicular vectors, an oriented piece of volume. What has come out is no longer a vector. That \(e_1e_2e_3\) cannot be one is shown by its square: it is \(-1\) (the calculation follows in Section 6), whereas by the second requirement every vector squares to a positive number. In the plane this was not a problem, because there every vector lies in the plane of rotation; in space there are directions that do not lie in it, and for these, one-sided multiplication is the wrong operation. Another route to rotations is therefore needed – and this route leads through mirrors.

Reflections

follows necessarily Let \(n\) be a unit vector. Every vector \(v\) can be split into a component \(v_\parallel\) parallel to \(n\) and a component \(v_\perp\) perpendicular to it. By Section 3, the parallel component commutes with \(n\), while the perpendicular one anticommutes. We are looking for an operation that reverses \(v_\parallel\) and leaves \(v_\perp\) alone. The rules offer exactly one difference between the two components: precisely this behaviour when swapped with \(n\). It becomes visible when \(v\) is multiplied by \(n\) from both sides: the left \(n\) then has to get past \(v\) in order to merge with the right one into \(nn = |n|^2 = 1\), and only the perpendicular component picks up a minus sign on the way. With an extra minus sign in front, this gives

\[ -\,n\,v\,n = -\,n\,v_\parallel\, n - n\,v_\perp\, n = -\,v_\parallel\,(nn) + v_\perp\,(nn) = -\,v_\parallel + v_\perp . \]

The component along \(n\) is reversed, the component perpendicular to it stays. This is exactly the reflection in the plane perpendicular to \(n\) – a mirror whose position is fixed by its normal vector \(n\). A reflection can therefore be written as a sandwich, and the result is guaranteed to be a vector again.

follows necessarily Now a fact comes into play that can be observed with any pair of mirrors: two reflections in succession produce a rotation, namely by twice the angle between the two mirrors. It can be checked in one line. In the plane containing the two normals, each mirror cuts out a line. A reflection in a line at the angle \(\beta\) maps an arrow at the angle \(\varphi\) to the angle \(2\beta - \varphi\). Two reflections in succession give \(2\beta_2 - (2\beta_1 - \varphi) = \varphi + 2(\beta_2 - \beta_1)\): every arrow is rotated by twice the angle between the mirrors, independently of \(\varphi\). Components perpendicular to this plane lie in both mirrors and stay unchanged. If \(v\) is reflected first in the mirror with normal \(m\), then in the mirror with normal \(n\), the result is

\[ v'' = -\,n\,(-\,m\,v\,m)\,n = (nm)\; v\; (mn) = R\,v\,\tilde R, \qquad R = nm, \quad \tilde R = mn . \]

The tilde in \(\tilde R\) (read “R tilde”) denotes the same product with the order of its factors reversed: \(nm\) becomes \(mn\). Then \(R\tilde R = n\,(mm)\,n = nn = 1\), and likewise \(\tilde R R = 1\).

Try it: Change the angle \(\alpha\) between the two mirrors and observe by what angle \(v''\) is rotated relative to \(v\). The dial on the right shows two hands: the rotation of the vector and the rotor \(R\). What happens at \(\alpha = 180^\circ\)?

The object \(R = nm\) is called a rotor. As the product of two unit vectors it is, by Section 3, a number plus an area: \(R = n\cdot m + n\wedge m\). If the mirrors enclose the angle \(\alpha\), then \(n\cdot m = \cos\alpha\) and \(n \wedge m = -\,B\sin\alpha\), where \(B\) is the unit area of the plane, traversed from \(m\) to \(n\). Since the rotation has the angle \(\theta = 2\alpha\), the rotor reads

\[ R = \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2} - B\,\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}, \qquad v' = R\,v\,\tilde R . \]

The rotation runs in the sense from \(m\) to \(n\), that is, in the sense of \(B\). The minus sign does not contradict Section 4. To the right of \(v\) stands \(\tilde R = \cos\tfrac{\theta}{2} + B\sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\), exactly the rotation instruction of Section 4, only with half the angle. To the left stands its counterpart with the minus sign, and for a vector \(v\) in the plane of rotation, which anticommutes with \(B\), \(R\,v = v\,\tilde R\). Hence \(R\,v\,\tilde R = v\,\tilde R^{\,2} = v\,(\cos\theta + B\sin\theta)\): the two half angles add up to the full one.

follows necessarily Here the half angle appears, and it is not a computational convention. It arises because two mirrors that produce a rotation by \(\theta\) enclose only the angle \(\theta/2\). Nor is the sandwich an additional choice: once reflections are recognised as \(-nvn\), it arises from two reflections by itself, and it guarantees that a vector turns into a vector again. The problem from above is solved as well. If \(e_1\) is perpendicular to the plane of rotation \(e_2e_3\), it commutes with \(e_2e_3\), because it has to be swapped past both factors and the two minus signs cancel. Hence \(e_1\) also commutes with \(R\), and \(R\,e_1\tilde R = e_1 R\tilde R = e_1\): the axis of rotation stays where it is.

A full turn, and yet \(R = -1\)

follows necessarily At \(\alpha = 180^\circ\) the widget shows a peculiarity that will be decisive for the rest of this post. If the second mirror is turned a further 180°, it lies in the same plane as before – a mirror has no front and back. Its normal vector, however, now points in the opposite direction, \(n \to -n\), and therefore \(R \to -R\). Nothing changes for the vector, because \(R\) appears twice in the sandwich, and the two minus signs cancel. Consequently \(R\) and \(-R\) describe the same rotation. Which of the two rotors belongs to a particular rotation is nevertheless not arbitrary, because it is fixed by the way there. If a rotation is followed continuously from the angle 0, the rotor starts at \(R = \cos 0 = +1\) and changes according to the formula above without any jump. For a full turn through \(\theta = 360^\circ\) it arrives at

\[ R = \cos 180^\circ - B\sin 180^\circ = -1 . \]

After the full turn the arrow is back in its original position, but the rotor is not: it has moved continuously from \(+1\) to \(-1\). Only a second turn brings it back continuously to \(+1\), after \(720^\circ\) in total. For vectors this difference is invisible, because \(R\) occurs twice in the sandwich. Whether there are objects for which it is visible – objects that are rotated not by the sandwich but one-sidedly by \(R\) – is a question that Act III will answer. For vectors, one-sided multiplication was the wrong operation above, because it leads out of the set of vectors. For objects that themselves have the form number plus area, however, it is the natural one: rotor times rotor is again a rotor. The answer is yes, and every electron is such an object.

Hamilton’s quaternions

A rotor in space consists of a number and an area, and an arbitrary area is a combination of the three basis areas. A rotor therefore has four components:

\[ R = a + b\,e_3e_2 + c\,e_1e_3 + d\,e_2e_1 . \]

Exactly these objects were found by William Rowan Hamilton on 16 October 1843, on a walk along the Royal Canal in Dublin. For years he had been searching for “numbers for space” that would do for three dimensions what the complex numbers do for the plane, and he had always calculated with three components. At Broom Bridge he realised that he needed four, and he carved the rule into a stone of the bridge:

\[ i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1 . \]

In retrospect, geometry explains why three components could not suffice. Hamilton calculated with a number and two imaginary units. But the product of two different basis areas is a third basis area, for instance \(e_1e_2\,e_2e_3 = e_1e_3\), and the product of an area with itself is a number. Only a number together with all three areas forms a set of objects that stays among itself under multiplication. That nevertheless only three quantities can be chosen freely, as for a rotation in space (two for the orientation of the plane, one for the angle), is due to \(R\tilde R = 1\): worked out, this gives \(a^2 + b^2 + c^2 + d^2 = 1\). Hamilton’s \(i\), \(j\), \(k\) are the three basis areas of space. convention The assignment does, however, involve a choice. With \(i = e_3e_2\), \(j = e_1e_3\) and \(k = e_2e_1\), Hamilton’s rules hold exactly; the assignment that seems more natural at first glance, \(i = e_2e_3\), \(j = e_3e_1\), \(k = e_1e_2\), gives \(ij = -k\) instead, that is, the mirror-image variant. Both algebras are equivalent; Hamilton opted for one of them.

Try it: The same two moves as in Section 4 are enough to verify Hamilton’s bridge formula.

To this day, quaternions are the standard whenever rotations in space have to be computed reliably: in the attitude control of space probes and satellites, in drones, in robots and in almost every 3D engine for computer games. The reason is the same one that produced them here. A rotor describes a rotation by a plane and an angle, without the detour of three successive rotations about fixed axes, and thereby avoids the singularities that arise with such angle decompositions.

Section 6

The cross product unmasked

At the beginning of this act, the cross product stood as the second unsatisfactory product. With the concepts of the previous sections it is now possible to say exactly what it is. follows necessarily The product of the three basis vectors, \(I = e_1e_2e_3\), is an oriented unit volume. The two familiar rules give \(I^2 = -1\): in \(e_1e_2e_3\,e_1e_2e_3\) the second \(e_1\) has to get past \(e_3\) and \(e_2\), two swaps with two minus signs, so \(I^2 = e_1e_1\,e_2e_3\,e_2e_3 = (e_2e_3)^2 = -1\). Likewise \(I\) commutes with every basis vector and hence with every vector, because a basis vector always has to get past the two other factors, and two minus signs cancel. Multiplying a basis area by \(I\) produces the vector perpendicular to it; for example, \(-\,I\,e_1e_2 = e_3\). Hence, in general,

\[ u \times v = -\,I\,(u \wedge v) . \]

Pictured intuitively, this means: the cross product is the area \(u\wedge v\), replaced by its normal vector. This replacement is possible only in three-dimensional space, because only there does a plane have exactly one perpendicular direction. In the plane there is none, in four dimensions a whole plane of them. Here, too, lies the reason for the shortcomings from Section 1: the cross product exists only in three dimensions because it disguises an area as an arrow, and it loses information because it omits the scalar part of the geometric product. convention Which of the two possible normal directions the arrow points in is fixed by the right-hand rule – an agreement that is reflected in the sign of \(I\).

This has physical consequences. Quantities traditionally written as cross products, such as angular momentum \(L = r \times p\) or torque, are by their nature areas: they describe in which plane and in which sense something rotates. That they react differently under reflection from “genuine” arrows – in physics they are called axial vectors – is, from this point of view, not a curiosity but the consequence of their not being arrows at all. The sandwich shows it directly: if \(u\) and \(v\) are reflected in the same mirror, \(uv\) becomes \((-nun)(-nvn) = n\,uv\,n\), and the area \(u\wedge v\) turns into \(n\,(u\wedge v)\,n\) – without the minus sign that a vector picks up under reflection. And in four-dimensional spacetime, where Act III takes place, there are \(4\cdot 3/2 = 6\) basis areas – exactly as many components as the electric and magnetic fields have together. This is no coincidence: in a full treatment, which this post does not carry out, the electromagnetic field turns out to be an area of spacetime.

From these followed necessarily the dot product, the oriented area, the anticommutation of perpendicular vectors, division by vectors, the object \(i\) as the unit square of the plane, rotation by multiplication, reflections as sandwiches, the rotor with its half angle, and the fact that a full turn has the rotor \(-1\). Section 7

Phasors that rotate and add

Act I was pure mathematics. With Act II nature enters the stage, and with it the kind of justification changes. From two requirements on a product it was possible to derive that areas square to a negative number; that the world behaves quantum-mechanically, by contrast, cannot be derived but only observed. The following sections therefore mark with particular care which steps come from experiments and which follow necessarily from them.

input The experiment. If electrons are sent one at a time, one after another, through a screen with two slits, a striped pattern builds up point by point on the detector behind it. There are places where hardly any electron arrives, even though electrons do arrive there as soon as one of the two slits is closed. In 1989 Akira Tonomura and colleagues at Hitachi filmed how this pattern builds up from individual impacts; with light, the effect had been known since Thomas Young’s experiments around 1801.

follows necessarily This observation has a far-reaching consequence. If the two slits were two routes, each with its own probability, then opening the second slit would have to increase the probability of arriving at a given point, not decrease it. Probabilities are numbers between 0 and 1, and two such numbers can never cancel each other when added. Consequently each route must be assigned something that can cancel: a quantity with a direction, for which two opposite copies add up to zero.

input Feynman’s rule. Richard Feynman described quantum mechanics in a form that needs exactly one such quantity, and in his book QED (1985) he explained it without a single formula. Every way in which an event can come about is assigned a phasor, an arrow in a plane. If a possibility consists of several steps in succession, the phasors of the steps are multiplied – in the sense of Act I, that is, angles of rotation are added and lengths multiplied. If there are several possibilities, their phasors are added, head to tail. The probability of the event is the square of the length of the resulting phasor. This rule is a summary of experiments, not a derivation, and it is named here as an input. That the phasors are precisely the rotation-scalings of the plane – complex numbers, that is, number plus area – is likewise a fact of experience. The last part of the rule, probability equals length squared, was formulated by Max Born in 1926; in 1957 Andrew Gleason showed that, under very weak assumptions, it is the only consistent possibility.

How this rule explains the double slit, diffraction and the refraction of light is shown in detail in the post on quantum physics with arrows. Here only one property of the phasors is needed, and it was already laid down in Act I: multiplying means rotating.

Many small rotations

follows necessarily A rotation through a very small angle \(\theta/N\) is approximately multiplication by \(1 + i\,\theta/N\), because for small angles \(\cos x \approx 1\) and \(\sin x \approx x\): the phasor keeps almost all of its length and gains a small increment turned through 90°. Performing \(N\) such steps one after another gives \((1 + i\theta/N)^N\). For \(\theta = 2\), for example, one obtains:

\(N\)\((1 + 2i/N)^N\)

For finite \(N\) the phasor grows slightly longer in the process, because \(|1 + i\theta/N|\) is minimally greater than 1; in the limit this error vanishes, and what remains is a pure rotation through \(\theta\). It is the same limit that Jakob Bernoulli found in 1683 when studying compound interest, and which leads to the number \(e\) (see the post on Euler’s number). Bernoulli worked with real numbers; for an imaginary exponent the exponential function is defined by the same limit, and the table shows that the limit is the rotation through \(\theta\):

\[ e^{i\theta} = \lim_{N\to\infty}\Big(1 + \frac{i\theta}{N}\Big)^N = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta . \]

This also redeems the notation from Section 4: the rotation instruction \(\cos\theta + e_1e_2\sin\theta\) is \(e^{e_1e_2\theta}\), a rotation composed of infinitely many infinitely small rotations. This pattern – many small steps that multiply together into an exponential function – will return in Section 11, then with matrices instead of numbers.

Section 8

Energy and momentum are rates of rotation

The simplest conceivable quantum system has only a single state and therefore only a single phasor. According to Feynman’s rule, the only thing that can be measured about it is its length squared, and if nothing happens, that length stays the same. Such a state is called stationary. The only freedom left to it is the direction of its phasor.

input How this direction can change over time is fixed by a single assumption that hardly anyone will dispute: no moment in time is distinguished from any other. follows necessarily Then a time step of duration \(s\) must always turn the phasor on by the same factor \(z(s)\), regardless of when it takes place, and two time steps in succession give \(z(t + s) = z(t)\,z(s)\). It follows that \(z(nt) = z(t)^n\) for every natural number \(n\): the function behaves like a power with time in the exponent. Indeed, the only continuous functions with this property are exponential functions, and since the length is preserved, only a uniform rotation comes into question:

\[ z(t) = e^{-i\omega t} . \]

This means, as a picture: the phasor of a stationary state runs round a circle at constant speed \(\omega\). On its own this rotation cannot be measured, since only the length is measured. It becomes observable only when components with different rates of rotation are superposed; then their difference counts, as Section 10 will show. convention That the exponent carries a minus sign, so that the phasor runs clockwise, is an agreement within physics; the other sign yields the same physics.

follows necessarily Differentiating this phasor with respect to time, with \(z = \cos\omega t - i\sin\omega t\) and the school rules of differentiation, gives \(dz/dt = -\omega\sin\omega t - i\omega\cos\omega t = -i\omega\,(\cos\omega t - i\sin\omega t)\), that is

\[ \frac{dz}{dt} = -\,i\,\omega\, z . \]

The change of the phasor is the phasor itself, turned through 90° clockwise (that is multiplication by \(-i\)) and stretched by the rate of rotation. This is the core of what will be needed from here on: differentiating extracts the rate of rotation.

input Which physical quantity this rate of rotation is, only experiment can reveal. Max Planck found in 1900, in thermal radiation, and Albert Einstein in 1905, in the photoelectric effect, that energy and frequency are proportional to each other. The constant of proportionality is the reduced Planck constant \(\hbar \approx 1.055 \cdot 10^{-34}\,\text{J\,s}\):

\[ E = \hbar\,\omega . \]

This means, as a picture: energy is rate of rotation, and \(\hbar\) is the conversion factor between the rate of rotation, measured in radians per second, and the energy in joules. If one counts full revolutions per second instead, \(\nu = \omega/2\pi\), the same relation reads \(E = h\nu\) with the original Planck constant \(h = 2\pi\hbar\). Substituting this, the differentiation rule becomes \(i\hbar\,dz/dt = E\,z\). That is the Schrödinger equation, for a system with only a single state.

Phasors along space

A particle that moves has not just one phasor but one at every location. If it has a definite energy and a definite momentum, each of these phasors rotates with \(\omega = E/\hbar\), and at a fixed moment the phasors along space are turned evenly against one another, with a “spatial rate of rotation” \(k\):

\[ \psi(x, t) = e^{\,i(kx - \omega t)} . \]

The different signs in front of \(kx\) and \(\omega t\) have a reason: the position \(kx - \omega t\) stays the same when \(x\) grows at the speed \(\omega/k\). For positive \(k\) the phasor pattern therefore moves to the right, and positive momentum means motion in the \(+x\) direction.

follows necessarily (with a result from Act III) That \(k\) is related to momentum in exactly the way \(\omega\) is related to energy, namely \(p = \hbar k\), was recognised by Louis de Broglie in 1924 with an argument resting on relativity alone. The position \(kx - \omega t\) of a phasor counts how often it has turned, and a count must be the same for every observer. A moving observer mixes time and place; for the position to stay the same, he has to mix the two rates of rotation \(\omega\) and \(k\) in a matching way. Section 12 will show that he mixes energy and momentum in exactly the same way. In the rest frame of the particle the momentum is zero and the row of phasors is not twisted along space, \(k = 0\); there \((\omega, k)\) and \((E, p)\) stand in the ratio \(1/\hbar\). Since both pairs are mixed in the same way when the observer changes, this ratio holds in every frame: if the temporal rate of rotation is \(E/\hbar\), the spatial one is \(p/\hbar\). In 1927 Clinton Davisson and Lester Germer confirmed the prediction by diffracting electrons off a nickel crystal.

Try it: The row of phasors shows \(\psi(x,t)\) at different locations. Change energy and momentum, and display what differentiating with respect to time and to position does to the phasors.

follows necessarily The widget shows the two derivatives side by side. Differentiated with respect to time, each phasor is turned 90° clockwise and stretched by \(E/\hbar\); differentiated with respect to position, it is turned 90° anticlockwise and stretched by \(p/\hbar\):

\[ i\hbar\,\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = E\,\psi, \qquad -\,i\hbar\,\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x} = p\,\psi . \]

The curly \(\partial\) means: differentiate with respect to one quantity while holding the other fixed. \(\partial\psi/\partial t\) is the change at a fixed place, \(\partial\psi/\partial x\) the change along space at a fixed moment. These two lines are the famous “translation rule” of quantum mechanics: energy corresponds to the time derivative, momentum to the spatial derivative. Textbooks usually introduce it as a postulate. Here it is earned – it follows from the fact that energy and momentum are rates of rotation and that differentiating extracts rates of rotation. For a state that has no single \(E\) and \(p\), it holds component by component, because every state can be written as a superposition of such rows of phasors – this is the Fourier decomposition, which is treated at length in the quantum post.

With this, three symbols of Dirac’s line are earned. \(\hbar\) is the conversion factor between rate of rotation and energy. \(\partial_\mu\) collects the derivatives with respect to time and the three directions of space, which extract the rates of rotation; the index \(\mu\) counts off these four directions (Section 15 writes them out). And \(\psi\) is the state: an assignment that gives every location a phasor. This phasor does not point in a direction of space but lives in a plane of its own; only its length is measurable, and a common rotation of all phasors through the same angle changes not a single prediction.

Section 9

A time step is a matrix

Let a system now have several states, for example three locations at which a particle can be found. Its state is then a list of three phasors, \(\psi = (\psi_1, \psi_2, \psi_3)\), one per location. What happens in a small time step? The particle can stay where it is or move to another location, and every such transition “from \(j\) to \(i\)” is assigned a phasor, denoted \(U_{ij}\).

follows necessarily Feynman’s rule determines how the new state follows from this. To be at location \(i\) after one step, the particle must have come from some location \(j\). The possibility “first at \(j\), then to \(i\)” consists of two steps, so their phasors are multiplied: \(U_{ij}\,\psi_j\). The different places of origin are different possibilities, so their phasors are added:

\[ \psi_i' = \sum_j U_{ij}\,\psi_j \qquad\text{in short:}\qquad \psi' = U\psi . \]

As in Section 8, no moment in time is distinguished; therefore every time step is the same matrix \(U\). The formula on the left is, word for word, the definition of the product of a matrix and a vector. Matrix multiplication is therefore not brought in here as a tool; it emerges from the rule “multiply along, add over alternatives”.

Key point: Matrix multiplication is the summing over all possibilities of a step.

Historically the discovery ran in the opposite direction. In 1925, on Heligoland, Werner Heisenberg set up a calculational rule for the lines in atomic spectra without knowing that it already existed in mathematics. Max Born recognised shortly afterwards that Heisenberg’s curious multiplication, in which the order matters, is the multiplication of matrices. Quantum mechanics began as matrix mechanics.

follows necessarily \(U\) must satisfy one condition. By Born’s rule the probabilities are the squared lengths of the phasors, and they must add up to 1 at every moment. Consequently \(U\) must not change the total length of the list of phasors, for any initial state. Matrices with this property are called unitary. In the space of phasor lists they are what the rotations of Act I are in ordinary space: length-preserving maps.

The path route

follows necessarily After \(N\) time steps, \(\psi(N) = U^N\psi(0)\). Writing out the matrix power, each of its entries is a sum:

\[ (U^N)_{ij} = \sum_{a,\,b,\,\dots,\,c} U_{ia}\,U_{ab}\cdots U_{cj} . \]

Each term belongs to a chain \(j \to c \to \dots \to b \to a \to i\), that is, to a path through the states, and its value is the product of the phasors along that path. The sum runs over all conceivable paths. This is the path integral in its simplest form: matrix multiplication, carried out \(N\) times in succession. In the limit of infinitely many, infinitely small time steps and infinitely many locations, it becomes Feynman’s sum over all histories, which he published in 1948.

Try it: On the left, all paths from location 1 to location 3 are drawn. Add the paths one by one and watch where the chain of their phasors ends.

For three locations and three time steps there are already nine paths, for four time steps 27, and their number grows exponentially. Each individual path contributes a phasor, and only the sum of all the phasors gives the amplitude. The tip of the chain always lands exactly on the entry of the matrix power – not approximately but exactly, because both calculations merely arrange the same sum differently.

Section 10

Two routes, one result

There is a second way to compute \(U^N\), and it goes back to Section 8. There, a single phasor rotating uniformly was the simplest of all systems. Are there states that \(U\) does not mix up but only rotates? Such states are called eigenstates, and for them \(Uv = \lambda v\) holds with a number \(\lambda\). follows necessarily Since \(U\) preserves length, \(|\lambda| = 1\): the eigenvalue is a pure rotation. For an eigenstate, applying \(U\) \(N\) times is trivial, \(U^Nv = \lambda^Nv\). For unitary matrices it has been proved that there are enough eigenstates to write every state as a sum of them (the spectral theorem). Because \(U(v + w) = Uv + Uw\), each component is rotated on its own, at its own rate, and at the end the components are put back together. This calculation is called diagonalising, because written in its eigenstates \(U\) has entries only on the diagonal.

That the path route and the eigenvalue route must give the same result is clear, since both compute \(U^N\). How the two fit together in detail is less obvious. The quantum post on this blog sets the two viewpoints side by side as two languages of the same physics; in an example with only two states, the agreement can be checked completely by hand.

A particle between two wells

The ammonia molecule \(\text{NH}_3\) has the shape of a flat pyramid: three hydrogen atoms form the base, the nitrogen atom the apex. The apex can lie above or below the base, and both positions are energetically equivalent – two wells of equal depth. Classically, the nitrogen atom would have to climb over an energy barrier to get from one position to the other; quantum-mechanically it tunnels through. This back-and-forth is the basis of the ammonia maser, with which Charles Townes built the forerunner of the laser in 1954.

follows necessarily In each time step the system stays in its well with the phasor \(a\) or hops into the other with the phasor \(b\). Since both wells are equivalent, the same holds in both directions, and the time step is

\[ U = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix} . \]

follows necessarily Unitarity constrains \(a\) and \(b\) severely. Applied to the state \((1, 0)\), \(U\) gives the state \((a, b)\); its length is preserved only if \(|a|^2 + |b|^2 = 1\). Applied to \((1, 1)\), \(U\) gives \((a+b)\,(1, 1)\), so \(|a+b| = 1\) as well. Regarding the phasors \(a\) and \(b\) as arrows enclosing the angle \(\gamma\), \(|a+b|^2 = |a|^2 + |b|^2 + 2|a|\,|b|\cos\gamma\) (Section 2), and this equals 1 again only for \(\cos\gamma = 0\). The phasors \(a\) and \(b\) are therefore perpendicular to each other. The hop phasor is therefore necessarily turned through 90° relative to the stay phasor. convention Since a common rotation of all phasors changes nothing, \(a\) may be chosen real; then

\[ a = \cos\varphi, \qquad b = -\,i\sin\varphi , \]

where \(\varphi\) may be positive or negative; the sign decides which of the two eigenstates below has the lower energy.

The path route. A path of \(N\) steps with exactly \(k\) hops has the phasor \(a^{N-k}b^k\), and there are \(\binom{N}{k}\) such paths, because out of \(N\) steps one chooses the \(k\) at which a hop occurs. The system ends up back in well 1 when \(k\) is even. The amplitude is therefore

\[ \psi_1(N) = \sum_{k\ \text{even}} \binom{N}{k}\, a^{N-k}\, b^k . \]

Here the binomial coefficients from the look back at Act I reappear, and for the same reason: what is being counted are choices.

The eigenvalue route. Which states does \(U\) not mix? The symmetric combination \((1, 1)\) – “equal in both wells” – turns into \((a+b)\,(1,1)\), the antisymmetric one \((1,-1)\) into \((a-b)\,(1,-1)\). These are the two eigenstates, with eigenvalues \(a+b\) and \(a-b\). The initial state \((1, 0)\) is half the sum of the two, and after \(N\) steps the amplitude in well 1 is therefore

\[ \psi_1(N) = \tfrac12\big[(a+b)^N + (a-b)^N\big] . \]

follows necessarily That the two expressions are equal is shown by the binomial theorem. \((a+b)^N\) contains all the terms \(\binom{N}{k}a^{N-k}b^k\), and \((a-b)^N\) the same ones, but with a minus sign for odd \(k\). In half the sum the odd terms cancel, and the even ones remain. Counting paths and diagonalising are two readings of the same formula.

Try it: On the left, the paths are grouped by their number of hops and added up; on the right, only the two eigen-components. Change \(\varphi\) and \(N\).

With \(a = \cos\varphi\) and \(b = -i\sin\varphi\), the eigenvalues are \(a \pm b = e^{\mp i\varphi}\): two phasors rotating at different rates, and after \(N\) steps \((a \pm b)^N = e^{\mp iN\varphi}\). Adding \(e^{-iN\varphi} = \cos N\varphi - i\sin N\varphi\) and \(e^{iN\varphi} = \cos N\varphi + i\sin N\varphi\), the sine parts cancel, and half the sum is \(\psi_1(N) = \cos(N\varphi)\). Since the probabilities add up to 1, the probability of finding the system in well 2 is \(1 - \cos^2(N\varphi) = \sin^2(N\varphi)\). The system thus oscillates back and forth between the wells. If a step lasts \(\varepsilon\), the two eigencomponents rotate at the rates \(\pm\varphi/\varepsilon\). The oscillation has the angular frequency \(2\varphi/\varepsilon\), the difference of the two rates of rotation, and by Section 8 that is the energy difference \(\Delta E\) of the two eigenstates divided by \(\hbar\). For ammonia \(\Delta E = h\nu\) with \(\nu = 23.87\) gigahertz, and the maser operates at precisely this frequency. This small \(2\times 2\) matrix, incidentally, will return at the end of Act III in a role that cannot be seen in it here.

Section 11

Multiplying out or diagonalising

An arbitrarily short time step may change the state only arbitrarily little. For very small time steps of duration \(\varepsilon\), \(U\) therefore differs only slightly from the identity matrix \(1\), which leaves every state unchanged. One writes

\[ U \approx 1 - \frac{i\varepsilon}{\hbar}\,H \]

with a matrix \(H\) called the Hamiltonian. follows necessarily The \(i\) in this formula is not decoration. To see this, one needs a notation for the length of a list of phasors. \(U^\dagger\) (read “U dagger”) denotes the matrix obtained by reflecting \(U\) in its diagonal and complex-conjugating every entry, that is, replacing \(i\) by \(-i\); likewise \(\psi^\dagger\) is the horizontal list of conjugated phasors. Then \(\psi^\dagger\psi = |\psi_1|^2 + |\psi_2|^2 + \dots\) is the squared length of the list, and after one step it is \((U\psi)^\dagger(U\psi) = \psi^\dagger U^\dagger U\psi\). It stays the same for every state if \(U^\dagger U = 1\); that is the unitarity of Section 9 in formulas. With the ansatz, \(U^\dagger \approx 1 + \tfrac{i\varepsilon}{\hbar}H^\dagger\), and multiplying out leaves, up to terms with \(\varepsilon^2\), only \(U^\dagger U \approx 1 + \tfrac{i\varepsilon}{\hbar}(H^\dagger - H)\). This equals 1 only if \(H = H^\dagger\), that is, if \(H\) is a so-called Hermitian matrix. Hermitian matrices have real eigenvalues: from \(Hv = Ev\) follows \(v^\dagger H v = E\,v^\dagger v\), and for Hermitian \(H\) the left-hand side equals its own complex conjugate, so it is real. For such an eigenstate one step is \(U v \approx (1 - i\varepsilon E/\hbar)\,v\), by Section 7 a small rotation through the angle \(\varepsilon E/\hbar\): exactly the rotation factor of Section 8 with \(\omega = E/\hbar\). The eigenvalues of \(H\) are therefore the energies of the system, and the \(i\) ensures that they appear as rates of rotation.

follows necessarily After \(N = t/\varepsilon\) steps the time evolution is \(U^N = (1 - i\varepsilon H/\hbar)^N\), and in the limit of small steps the same limit as in Section 7 results, only with a matrix instead of a number. For a matrix \(M\), \(e^{M}\) is defined by precisely this limit \((1 + M/N)^N\):

\[ U(t) = e^{-iHt/\hbar} . \]

From \(\psi(t+\varepsilon) = (1 - i\varepsilon H/\hbar)\,\psi(t)\) one also obtains, dividing by \(\varepsilon\) and letting \(\varepsilon\) go to zero, the equation of motion

\[ i\hbar\,\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = H\psi , \]

the time-dependent Schrödinger equation for any number of states; Section 8 had its special case with a single state. The eigenstates of \(H\) are the states that only rotate: if \(Hv = Ev\), every factor \((1 - i\varepsilon H/\hbar)\) acts on \(v\) like the number \((1 - i\varepsilon E/\hbar)\), and \(U(t)\,v = e^{-iEt/\hbar}\,v\) follows. The eigenvalue equation

\[ Hv = E\,v \]

is the time-independent Schrödinger equation. Erwin Schrödinger published it in 1926 under the title Quantisierung als Eigenwertproblem (“Quantisation as an Eigenvalue Problem”) – the allowed energies of an atom are the eigenvalues of a matrix or, for infinitely many locations, of a differential operator. How eigenvalues turn up far beyond physics is shown in the post on eigenvalues and AI.

Key point: The path integral multiplies \(e^{-iHt/\hbar}\) out. The eigenvalue calculation diagonalises it. Both compute the same object.

Looking back: Act II

Inputs from experiment: probabilities can cancel (double slit); every possibility is assigned a phasor, phasors are multiplied along a route and added over alternatives; probability is length squared (Born); no moment in time is distinguished; energy is proportional to frequency (Planck, Einstein).

Followed necessarily: stationary states rotate uniformly; differentiating extracts the rate of rotation; momentum is spatial rate of rotation (de Broglie, with a result from Act III); from this the translation rule \(E \leftrightarrow i\hbar\,\partial_t\), \(p \leftrightarrow -i\hbar\,\partial_x\); a time step is a matrix, and it must be unitary; matrix powers are sums over paths; eigenstates only rotate; path route and eigenvalue route agree; \(H\) is Hermitian and its eigenvalues are the energies; the Schrödinger equation \(i\hbar\,\partial\psi/\partial t = H\psi\).

Conventions: the minus sign in \(e^{-i\omega t}\); the choice of a real stay phasor \(a\).

What is still missing is relativity. It will demand that time and space be treated on an equal footing – and that leads to a square root that can only be taken with the geometry of Act I.

Dirac’s Square Root

Guiding question: How does one take the square root of \(E^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4\)?

Section 12

Space and time must be on an equal footing

With the translation rule from Act II, a quantum equation can be written down for every energy formula of classical physics. In 1926 Erwin Schrödinger did exactly that with the energy of a free particle, \(E = p^2/2m\). follows necessarily Replacing \(E\) by \(i\hbar\,\partial_t\) and \(p\) by \(-i\hbar\,\partial_x\) yields

\[ i\hbar\,\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = -\,\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2} . \]

Because the energy appears to the first power in the formula and the momentum is squared, the equation differentiates once with respect to time and twice with respect to position. For slow particles this equation is superbly confirmed; the whole of chemistry rests on it. For fast particles, however, it cannot be right, and the reason is an experiment.

input Light travels at the same speed for everyone. In 1887 Albert Michelson and Edward Morley found no difference whatsoever in the speed of light, regardless of the direction in which the Earth happened to be moving along its orbit. In 1905 Einstein turned this into a principle: every observer measures the same speed \(c\) for light, no matter how fast he himself is moving.

follows necessarily It follows that observers in motion relative to one another must divide up time and position differently – much as two maps that are rotated against each other divide up east and north differently. For the map, rotation leaves the distance from the origin unchanged, that is \(x^2 + y^2\). In spacetime something else must stay unchanged. A flash of light that starts at time \(0\) at position \(0\) is at position \(x = ct\) at time \(t\), and because all observers measure the same speed for it, \((ct)^2 - x^2 = 0\) holds for all of them. The quantity that is the same for every observer is therefore not \((ct)^2 + x^2\) but

\[ (ct)^2 - x^2 . \]

input So far this only shows that the value zero is the same for all observers. That every other value is preserved too follows from Einstein’s second principle, the principle of relativity: no observer is distinguished from any other. The conversion to a moving observer and back again must therefore not stretch anything, and the expression stays the same for every event. Where the map has a plus sign, spacetime has a minus sign. This means, intuitively: when one switches to a different observer, the coordinates of an event do not move along a circle but along a hyperbola.

Try it: Compare how the coordinates of a fixed point move when you rotate a map and when you change the velocity of an observer. The third mode shows the same for the energy and momentum of a particle.

The widget makes clear what an equation has to achieve if it is to hold for all observers. When a map is rotated, the expression \(x^2 + y^2\) survives every rotation, whereas the expression \(x + y^2\) does not, because it treats one direction differently from the other. follows necessarily Schrödinger’s equation is of the type \(x + y^2\): it differentiates once with respect to time and twice with respect to position. In another frame of reference it therefore looks different, and it cannot hold for all observers at once.

What holds for time and position holds equally for energy and momentum, and this can be justified. In the short time \(dt\) a particle covers the distance \(dx\). The pair \((c\,dt, dx)\) is a small piece of a spacetime arrow and is mixed under a change of observer exactly like \((ct, x)\). The quantity \((c\,d\tau)^2 = (c\,dt)^2 - dx^2\) is the same for everyone; \(d\tau\) is the time shown by a clock carried along with the particle, its proper time. input In relativity, energy and momentum are defined as \(E/c = mc\,dt/d\tau\) and \(p = m\,dx/d\tau\), that is, as the pair \((c\,dt, dx)\), divided by the number \(d\tau\) that is the same for everyone and multiplied by the mass \(m\). For slow particles \(d\tau \approx dt\), and the familiar momentum \(p \approx m\,dx/dt\) results. follows necessarily Energy and momentum are therefore mixed exactly like time and position. An observer in motion measures a different energy and a different momentum for the same particle, but the combination \((E/c)^2 - p^2 = m^2\,\big((c\,dt)^2 - dx^2\big)/d\tau^2 = m^2c^2\) is the same for everyone. For a particle at rest \(dx = 0\) and \(dt = d\tau\), so \(p = 0\) and \(E = mc^2\). In general, Einstein’s energy formula holds:

\[ E^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4 . \]

This means, intuitively: energy and momentum form an arrow in spacetime, and its “length” – measured with the minus sign of spacetime – is fixed by the mass: in the components \((E/c, p)\) it is \(mc\). Unlike Schrödinger’s formula, this equation treats energy and momentum alike: both appear squared. With this, the symbol \(c\) is already half earned. It is the conversion factor that makes time and position measurable in the same unit, so that \(ct\) is a length and \(pc\) is an energy.

Section 13

Schrödinger’s discarded equation

The obvious next step is to translate Einstein’s formula directly. With the rule from Act II, \(E^2\) becomes the second time derivative and \(p^2\) the second spatial derivative:

\[ -\,\hbar^2\,\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial t^2} = -\,\hbar^2c^2\,\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2} + m^2c^4\,\psi . \]

Here time and position are treated alike; each is differentiated twice. Today the equation is called the Klein–Gordon equation, after Oskar Klein and Walter Gordon, who published it in 1926. The first to find it, however, was Schrödinger himself, before he wrote down his famous equation. He set it aside at first because it predicted the wrong fine structure of the spectral lines for the hydrogen atom, and he published instead the non-relativistic version, which matched the measurements.

follows necessarily As an equation for the electron, the Klein–Gordon equation has a fundamental problem, and Act II allows us to name it precisely. There it turned out that a time step is a matrix: \(\psi' = U\psi\). The state at one moment therefore fixes the state at every later moment, and for that an equation that differentiates once with respect to time is sufficient. An equation that differentiates twice with respect to time, by contrast, needs two starting values – the state and its rate of change –, just as a thrown ball is fixed only by its position and its velocity. The state alone then no longer determines the future. There is a second problem. Because only \(E^2\) appears in the equation, it has for every momentum not only a solution with \(E = +\sqrt{p^2c^2 + m^2c^4}\) but also one with \(E = -\sqrt{p^2c^2 + m^2c^4}\). If one works out what plays the role of the probability of finding the particle in this equation (the calculation is not shown here), one obtains a quantity that becomes negative for these negative-energy solutions. A negative probability makes no sense.

In fairness it should be said that this does not make the Klein–Gordon equation wrong. It correctly describes particles without spin, such as pions; in 1934 Wolfgang Pauli and Victor Weisskopf showed that the problematic quantity is to be read as an electric charge density, which may perfectly well take either sign. As an equation for a single electron in the sense of Act II, however, it will not do.

This outlines Dirac’s problem of 1928, and each of its conditions has a reason:

  • follows necessarily First order in time, because the state alone is to determine the future (Act II).
  • follows necessarily First order in space, because time and position must be treated alike (Section 12).
  • follows necessarily Linear in \(\psi\), because phasors must be able to superpose (Feynman’s rule).
  • follows necessarily Compatible with Einstein: every solution must satisfy \(E^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4\), because this holds for every free particle.

What is wanted, then, is an equation in which the energy appears to the first power but which nevertheless satisfies Einstein’s quadratic formula. Formally this means \(E = \sqrt{p^2c^2 + m^2c^4}\). Writing the square root down as it stands does not help. It can indeed be defined with the help of the Fourier decomposition, but then the change of \(\psi\) at one point depends on the values of \(\psi\) throughout all of space – an action without time delay across arbitrary distances, which is exactly what the theory of relativity forbids. What is needed is a square root without a root sign.

Section 14

The square root of a sum of squares

Dirac’s problem can be reduced to a question that this post has already answered once. For simplicity, first with only two quantities: we seek two objects \(a\) and \(b\) such that for all numbers \(x\) and \(y\)

\[ (ax + by)^2 = x^2 + y^2 \]

holds. Then \(ax + by\) would be a square root of \(x^2 + y^2\), and indeed one in which \(x\) and \(y\) appear only to the first power. Multiplying out, without assuming that \(a\) and \(b\) commute, gives

\[ (ax+by)^2 = a^2\,x^2 + (ab + ba)\,xy + b^2\,y^2 . \]

follows necessarily For this to equal \(x^2+y^2\) for all \(x, y\), three conditions must hold: \(a^2 = 1\), \(b^2 = 1\) and \(ab + ba = 0\). With ordinary numbers this is impossible, because for them \(ab + ba = 2ab\), and that vanishes only if \(a\) or \(b\) is zero.

Try it: First try to satisfy the three conditions with numbers. Then with matrices. And then: is there a fourth?

follows necessarily The three conditions, however, are exactly the rules from the key-point box in Section 3: a unit vector squares to 1, and two perpendicular ones anticommute. The problem has therefore long since been solved, and the solution is \(a = e_1\), \(b = e_2\):

\[ (x\,e_1 + y\,e_2)^2 = x^2 + y^2 . \]

This is precisely the second requirement from Section 2, applied to the vector with components \(x\) and \(y\): a vector times itself gives its squared length. This means, intuitively: the square root of a sum of squares is a vector. What is impossible with numbers succeeds with the geometric product without any additional assumption.

Four directions for spacetime

For Einstein’s formula, four such objects are needed instead of two, one for time and three for space. They are denoted \(\gamma^0, \gamma^1, \gamma^2, \gamma^3\). follows necessarily We seek a square root of \(E^2/c^2 - p_x^2 - p_y^2 - p_z^2\), the squared length of the energy-momentum arrow from Section 12. Dirac thus takes the root not of \(E^2\) alone but of the squared length of the whole arrow, which is the same for all observers, namely \((mc)^2\); this keeps time and space on an equal footing. The form with \(E\) alone on one side returns in Section 17. By the second requirement, every basis vector must square to the squared length of its direction, and in spacetime that is \(+1\) for time and \(-1\) for space. Different basis vectors anticommute, as before. Written compactly:

\[ \gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu + \gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu = 2\,\eta^{\mu\nu}, \qquad \eta = \operatorname{diag}(+1,-1,-1,-1) . \]

Here \(\eta\) is the table of squared lengths: \(\eta^{00} = +1\) for time, \(\eta^{11} = \eta^{22} = \eta^{33} = -1\) for the three directions of space and \(0\) for two different directions, which anticommute. Here a circle closes that was opened in Section 2. It was remarked there that one could equally well have required \(vv = -|v|^2\), and that this freedom of sign would later be decided by physics. That has now happened: in spacetime the time direction squares positively and the space directions negatively, because the squared length of spacetime has these signs. The \(\gamma\) matrices are an orthonormal basis of spacetime – the same rules as for \(e_1, e_2, e_3\), only with the metric that the theory of relativity prescribes. convention Whether one gives time the plus and space the minus or the other way round is an agreement; physically, only the difference between the signs matters.

follows necessarily These rules also explain why observers in motion see coordinates move along hyperbolas rather than circles. The swap puzzle from Act I works with the \(\gamma\) just as it does with the \(e\) – except that the squares are now partly \(+1\) and partly \(-1\).

An area that connects two space directions squares to \(-1\), as in Act I, and generates ordinary rotations with cosine and sine. An area that connects time and space, by contrast, squares to \(+1\): \((\gamma^0\gamma^1)^2 = \gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^0\gamma^1 = -\,\gamma^0\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^1 = -(+1)(-1) = +1\). Repeating the reasoning of Section 7 with an object that squares to \(+1\) instead of \(-1\), the signs no longer alternate, and instead of cosine and sine the hyperbolic functions \(\cosh w = (e^w + e^{-w})/2\) and \(\sinh w = (e^w - e^{-w})/2\) appear. They satisfy \(\cosh^2 w - \sinh^2 w = 1\), the equation of a hyperbola. The rotation instruction from Section 4 becomes \(\cosh w + \gamma^0\gamma^1\sinh w\), and the points move along hyperbolas. The parameter \(w\) is called rapidity and is related to the observer’s velocity \(v\) by \(\tanh w = v/c\). Switching to an observer in motion is therefore a rotation in a time–space plane – the same algebra as the rotation of a map, only with the other sign.

Why they must be matrices – and why four by four

follows necessarily Ordinary numbers are out of the question for the \(\gamma^\mu\), because they commute. Dirac therefore needed objects that anticommute, and he found them in matrices. For three space directions they already existed: in 1927 Wolfgang Pauli had introduced three \(2\times2\) matrices \(\sigma_x, \sigma_y, \sigma_z\) to describe the spin of the electron, and they satisfy exactly the rules for \(e_1, e_2, e_3\). The Pauli matrices are a representation of the space from Act I. That they square to \(+1\), whereas the spatial \(\gamma^k\) are to square to \(-1\), is no obstacle: multiplying a matrix by \(i\) reverses its square, and anticommutation is preserved. All that matters is whether there are four matrices that anticommute in pairs.

follows necessarily For spacetime, however, a fourth direction is needed, and in \(2\times 2\) there is no fourth matrix that anticommutes with all three Pauli matrices. Every \(2\times2\) matrix can be written as a combination \(a_0\cdot 1 + a_1\sigma_x + a_2\sigma_y + a_3\sigma_z\), and such a combination anticommutes with all three \(\sigma\) only if all its coefficients are zero; the widget lets you try. \(3 \times 3\) is ruled out as well, and one notion from linear algebra suffices for that. The trace of a matrix is the sum of its diagonal entries; it is also the sum of the eigenvalues and does not change when \(A\) is replaced by \(BAB^{-1}\). Let \(A\) be one of the matrices sought with \(A^2 = 1\) (for square \(-1\) take \(i\) times it); then every eigenvalue of \(A\) is \(+1\) or \(-1\). Let \(B\) be a second one that anticommutes with \(A\); since \(B^2 = \pm 1\) it has an inverse, and \(AB = -BA\) gives \(BAB^{-1} = -A\). So the trace of \(A\) equals its own negative and is therefore zero: there are equally many eigenvalues \(+1\) and \(-1\), and the size of the matrix must be even. The smallest possibility after \(2\times2\) is \(4 \times 4\), and that it actually suffices is shown by the matrices written out below. And because the \(\gamma\) matrices must act on something, \(\psi\) can no longer be a single number: the state of an electron is a list of four phasors.

The \(\gamma\) matrices written out

In the representation that Dirac himself used, the four matrices, written in \(2\times2\) blocks, are

\[ \gamma^0 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \gamma^k = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \sigma_k \\ -\sigma_k & 0 \end{pmatrix} \quad (k = 1,2,3), \]

where \(1\) is the \(2\times2\) identity matrix and \(\sigma_k\) are the Pauli matrices. One checks that \((\gamma^0)^2 = +1\), \((\gamma^k)^2 = -1\), and that any two different ones anticommute. convention This particular choice is a convention. There are other, equivalent representations – named after Weyl or after Majorana – that arise from one another by a change of basis, just as two coordinate systems rotated against each other describe the same space. What counts physically are only the rules \(\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu + \gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu = 2\eta^{\mu\nu}\), not the numbers in the matrices.

Section 15

Reading the equation

Now Dirac’s line can be read symbol by symbol. With \(x^0 = ct\) and \(\partial_0 = \partial/\partial(ct)\), the expression

\[ \gamma^\mu\partial_\mu = \gamma^0\,\partial_0 + \gamma^1\,\partial_1 + \gamma^2\,\partial_2 + \gamma^3\,\partial_3 \]

stands for a sum of four derivatives, each multiplied by its basis vector – time and space treated entirely alike, each direction differentiated only once. The short form on the left follows an agreement, the summation convention: an index that appears both up and down is summed over; \(\partial_1, \partial_2, \partial_3\) are the derivatives with respect to \(x, y, z\). follows necessarily By the translation rule from Act II, \(i\hbar\,\partial_0 = (i\hbar/c)\,\partial_t\) yields the quantity \(E/c\), and \(i\hbar\,\partial_k\) yields \(-p_k\), because momentum corresponds to \(-i\hbar\,\partial_x\). Applied to a row of phasors with fixed \(E\) and \(p\), the expression \(i\hbar\,\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\) is therefore the arrow \(\gamma^0\,E/c - p_x\gamma^1 - p_y\gamma^2 - p_z\gamma^3\): the energy-momentum arrow of spacetime, written out with its four basis vectors. The minus sign in front of the momentum parts does not change its squared length.

Einstein’s formula says that this squared length is \((mc)^2\). Dirac demands more, namely that the arrow itself, applied to \(\psi\), simply gives \(mc\,\psi\) – a first-order equation that entails the quadratic formula, as shown in a moment. convention Whether one writes \(+mc\) or \(-mc\) is an agreement; both signs lead to the same physics. The equation reads

\[ \big(i\hbar\,\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - mc\big)\,\psi = 0 \]

says, intuitively: the energy-momentum arrow, applied to \(\psi\), gives the mass. follows necessarily Applying it twice gives, by the equation, \((mc)^2\psi\). On the other hand, the square can be worked out. Written briefly, the arrow is \(\gamma^0 a_0 + \gamma^1 a_1 + \gamma^2 a_2 + \gamma^3 a_3\) with ordinary numbers \(a_\mu\). Multiplying out produces the squares \((\gamma^\mu)^2 a_\mu^2\), and every mixed pair appears twice, as \((\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu + \gamma^\nu\gamma^\mu)\,a_\mu a_\nu\), which vanishes because of anticommutation – exactly as with \((ax+by)^2\) in Section 14. What remains is \(a_0^2 - a_1^2 - a_2^2 - a_3^2 = E^2/c^2 - p^2\). Equating gives \(E^2/c^2 - p^2 = m^2c^2\), which is Einstein’s formula. Every solution of the Dirac equation satisfies it, as the fourth condition from Section 13 demands – yet the equation itself differentiates only once with respect to time.

Each of the four components of \(\psi\) therefore satisfies, on its own, the Klein–Gordon equation from Section 13. This is no contradiction. That equation was not wrong but unusable as the sole fundamental equation, because it needs two starting values. The Dirac equation fixes the future from the state alone and still inherits Einstein’s formula.

With this, the last symbols are earned. \(\gamma^\mu\) are the four basis vectors of spacetime, the square root that Dirac was looking for. \(m\) fixes the length \(mc\) of the energy-momentum arrow. And \(c\) is the conversion factor that turns time into length, so that all four directions have the same unit. The equation bar at the top of the page is complete.

One remark on the symbol \(i\) still deserves space. In the equation it stands as the ordinary imaginary unit of the complex numbers, not as the area element \(e_1e_2\) from Act I, and therefore commutes with all the matrices. In 1966, in his Space-Time Algebra, David Hestenes showed that the Dirac equation can be written entirely in the geometric algebra of spacetime, without complex numbers, and that the role of \(i\) is then taken over by a particular area of spacetime – entirely in the spirit of Act I. (For the details the reader is referred to Hestenes’ work; what follows does not need this reformulation.)

Section 16

Spin ½: the half angle pays off

In Section 5 a question remained open. The rotor of a full turn is \(R = -1\), but vectors notice nothing of this, because they are rotated with the sandwich \(R\,v\,\tilde R\). Are there objects that are rotated with \(R\) from one side only, and for which the minus sign therefore remains visible? The Dirac equation answers this question, and it does so without anyone having to ask it.

input No direction in space is singled out. If one rotates the laboratory, that is, the coordinate system, the equation must therefore have the same form in the new system. follows necessarily In the equation the \(\gamma^\mu\) appear as basis vectors of spacetime, and vectors rotate with the sandwich. For the equation to take on the same shape again after the rotation, \(\psi\) must compensate for this rotation. This succeeds if \(\psi\) is multiplied by the rotor from one side. The arrow \(\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\) is turned by the sandwich into \(R\,\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\,\tilde R\), and with \(\psi \to R\psi\) this becomes \(R\,\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\,\tilde R R\,\psi = R\,\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\,\psi\), because \(\tilde R R = 1\). The whole equation is thus merely multiplied by \(R\) from the left and keeps its form. (That there is no other possibility is shown by a longer calculation not given here.) In short:

\[ \psi \;\longrightarrow\; R\,\psi . \]

This means: the state of an electron does not rotate like an arrow but like a rotor – with the half angle. After a full turn through \(360^\circ\), \(R = -1\), and the electron has passed into the state \(-\psi\). Only after \(720^\circ\) is it back in the state \(\psi\). Objects with this behaviour are called spinors, and this is the meaning of spin ½: the “half” is the half angle from Section 5.

input A minus sign in front of the entire state is not measurable on its own, because by Born’s rule only squared lengths count. It does become measurable, however, as soon as a rotated and an unrotated part of a state interfere with each other. Exactly this was achieved in 1975 by two groups independently of each other, led by Helmut Rauch in Vienna and by Samuel Werner in Missouri respectively. They split a neutron beam into two halves, let the spin of one half precess in a magnetic field and brought both halves back together. The interference pattern did not repeat after a rotation through \(360^\circ\), but only after \(720^\circ\).

That a rotation through \(360^\circ\) is not always “nothing” can also be experienced without quantum mechanics. Dirac is said to have demonstrated it to his students with a belt fastened at one end to an object and at the other to a wall.

Try it: Rotate the plate through \(360^\circ\) and try to untangle the band. Then through \(720^\circ\).

After one turn the band is twisted, and no movement of the band that keeps the wall and the plate fixed can remove the twist. After two turns the band is twisted twice over, yet it can be pulled smooth. The reason is the same as for spin. The state of the band at every height is a rotation, and the path of these rotations from the wall to the plate can be described as a chain of rotors. At \(360^\circ\) the chain begins at \(R = +1\) and ends at \(R = -1\). A continuous deformation cannot flip this sign at the end – there is no continuous transition from \(-1\) to \(+1\) as long as the plate stays still. At \(720^\circ\) the chain ends at \(+1\), and then it can be contracted continuously to “\(+1\) everywhere”. (The construction that the widget uses rests on the fact that a rotor of the form \(\cos 180^\circ - B\sin 180^\circ\) equals \(-1\) for every plane \(B\); two such factors give \(+1\), however the plane of the second is swung round.) The same can be demonstrated with a plate on the flat of one’s hand: after one turn the arm is twisted, after a second turn in the same direction it is relaxed again.

Before Dirac, spin was a foreign body. George Uhlenbeck and Samuel Goudsmit proposed it in 1925 to explain the splitting of spectral lines, and Pauli built it by hand into the Schrödinger equation in 1927. In Dirac’s work it follows from the equation itself. One qualification is in order here: Jean-Marc Lévy-Leblond showed in 1967 that even a non-relativistic first-order equation, “square-rooted” in the same way, yields spin ½. Spin therefore arises not from the theory of relativity as such, but from taking the square root with the geometric product – which strengthens rather than weakens the thesis of this post.

Section 17

What \(\psi\) really is

According to Section 14, the state of an electron is a list of four phasors at every point. What do these four mean? To find out, the Dirac equation is rearranged so that it takes the form from Act II, \(i\hbar\,\partial_t\psi = H\psi\). One multiplies it from the left by \(\gamma^0\), uses \((\gamma^0)^2 = 1\), solves for the time derivative and multiplies by \(c\). With \(p_k = -i\hbar\,\partial_k\) and the abbreviation \(\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf p = \alpha_1p_x + \alpha_2p_y + \alpha_3p_z\), the Hamiltonian then reads

\[ H = c\,\boldsymbol\alpha\cdot\mathbf p + \beta\,mc^2, \qquad \alpha_k = \gamma^0\gamma^k, \quad \beta = \gamma^0 . \]

follows necessarily The \(\alpha_k\) and \(\beta\) square to 1, for instance \(\alpha_1^2 = \gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^0\gamma^1 = -(\gamma^0)^2(\gamma^1)^2 = 1\), and anticommute in pairs – once again the rules from Act I, this time for four objects with positive square. Squaring therefore gives

\[ H^2 = \big(p^2c^2 + m^2c^4\big)\cdot 1 . \]

According to Section 11, the eigenvalues of \(H\) are the possible energies. Since \(H^2\) is a multiple of the identity matrix, every eigenvalue must satisfy \(E = \pm\sqrt{p^2c^2 + m^2c^4}\). For a row of phasors with fixed momentum, \(H\) is an ordinary \(4\times4\) matrix. Its trace is zero, because by the trace argument of Section 14 \(\alpha_k\) and \(\beta\) each have trace zero. The four eigenvalues therefore add up to zero, and that is only possible if each of the two signs occurs exactly twice. For every momentum there are four independent solutions: two signs of energy times two directions of spin. (The four components \(\psi_1, \dots, \psi_4\) themselves have this meaning only for a particle at rest in Dirac’s representation.)

What “direction of spin” means deserves a sentence of caution. The electron is not a spinning sphere. In 1925 Hendrik Lorentz showed Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit by calculation that the surface of such a sphere would have to rotate faster than light. Spin is rather the way \(\psi\) behaves under rotations, namely with the half angle, and its measurable consequence is an angular momentum that, along any chosen axis, takes exactly one of two values: \(+\hbar/2\) or \(-\hbar/2\). Why exactly half of \(\hbar\)? Just as energy is the phasor’s rate of rotation per time and momentum its rate per step in space (Section 8), angular momentum is the phasor’s rate of rotation per angle through which the laboratory is turned, in units of \(\hbar\). A spinor turns along with only half the angle, that is, at the rate \(\pm\tfrac12\), and that gives \(\pm\hbar/2\). In 1922 Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach had seen a beam of silver atoms split into exactly two partial beams in an inhomogeneous magnetic field. convention Which axis one chooses for the description is free; customarily it is the \(z\) axis.

follows necessarily One of the problems from Section 13 is solved as well. The Dirac equation yields as the probability of finding the particle the sum of the squared lengths of all four phasors, \(|\psi_1|^2 + |\psi_2|^2 + |\psi_3|^2 + |\psi_4|^2\), and as a sum of squared lengths this quantity is never negative. That its sum over all space is conserved follows as in Section 11: the matrices \(\alpha_k\) and \(\beta\) are Hermitian, hence so is \(H\), and the time evolution is unitary. Dirac’s insistence on a first-order equation has thus paid off. What remains, however, is the second sign of the energy, and it leads to the most astonishing prediction of the equation.

Section 18

The second sign: antimatter

follows necessarily Every square root has two signs, and the Dirac equation cannot throw either of them away: according to Section 17, it has for every momentum two solutions with positive energy \(E = +\sqrt{p^2c^2+m^2c^4}\) and two with negative energy, \(E = -\sqrt{p^2c^2+m^2c^4}\). The magnitude of the energy is smallest for \(p = 0\), where the two branches lie at \(+mc^2\) and \(-mc^2\). The two energy branches are therefore separated by a gap of width \(2mc^2\), which for the electron is about 1.02 megaelectronvolts.

The negative branch came as a shock. An electron at rest would have available to it not only \(E = +mc^2\) but also states with \(E = -mc^2\), and below that the energy ladder would continue without end, because \(E = -\sqrt{p^2c^2+m^2c^4}\) becomes arbitrarily negative as the momentum grows. According to everything that was known about atoms, an electron drops into a state of lower energy as soon as one is free, and emits the difference as light. Every electron in the universe would accordingly have to plunge into a bottomless abyss under constant radiation, and no atom would be stable.

input Dirac’s way out, in 1930, was bold, and it must be marked explicitly as an interpretation, not as a deduction. He took seriously Pauli’s exclusion principle of 1925, according to which every state can be occupied by at most one electron, and concluded: if all states of negative energy are already occupied, no electron can fall into them. The vacuum would then be not empty space but a completely filled sea of negative-energy electrons, which is the same everywhere and therefore remains invisible. Only a disturbance of this sea would be visible.

Try it: Send a photon into the sea. How much energy does it need for something to happen? Then apply an electric field and observe in which direction the hole moves.

follows necessarily If a light quantum lifts an electron out of the sea onto the positive branch, a hole is left behind. To do so, the light quantum must bridge at least the gap \(2mc^2\), and it needs an atomic nucleus nearby to take up the excess momentum; a single light quantum cannot create a pair in empty space. The hole is the absence of an electron with negative energy and negative charge, and the absence of something negative acts like something positive – just as an air bubble rises in water while the water falls. The hole therefore behaves like a particle with positive energy and positive charge. Its mass, too, follows from the calculation: the missing electron had the energy \(-\sqrt{p^2c^2+m^2c^4}\) with the same mass \(m\), so its absence acts like a particle with \(+\sqrt{p^2c^2+m^2c^4}\) – with the mass of the electron.

Dirac needed a detour to reach this conclusion. In 1930 he at first took the hole to be the proton, the only positively charged particle known at the time, and hoped that the large difference in mass could be explained later, perhaps by the interaction of the hole with the sea. Robert Oppenheimer objected in the same year that every hydrogen atom would then have to annihilate almost instantly, because electron and “hole” could destroy each other. Hermann Weyl showed in 1931, from considerations of symmetry, that the hole must have the same mass as the electron. Dirac adopted the argument and in the same year predicted a new particle, the “anti-electron”. Carl Anderson found it in August 1932 in cosmic radiation, and in 1933 Patrick Blackett and Giuseppe Occhialini observed in cloud-chamber photographs how electron and positron are created in pairs.

follows necessarily The reverse process is annihilation: an electron falls back into the hole, and both disappear. The energy \(2mc^2\) is released, for particles at rest as two light quanta of \(mc^2 = 511\) kiloelectronvolts each, which, because of the conservation of momentum, fly off in opposite directions. This is exactly the signal that the PET scanner from the prologue registers. The circle back to the beginning of this post is thereby closed.

The Dirac sea is no longer the way physicists talk about antimatter today. Ernst Stückelberg (1941) and Richard Feynman (1949) interpreted the negative-energy solutions in quantum field theory as antiparticles with positive energy, without an infinite sea of occupied states. The result that Dirac had obtained from his square root, however, has remained and holds generally: for every particle there is an antiparticle with the same mass and opposite charge. The antiproton was detected in Berkeley in 1955.

Section 19

What the equation explains – and where it ends

Antimatter and spin are the most famous consequences of the Dirac equation, but not the only ones. Two further ones show how much the line of 1928 explained at a single stroke.

The magnetic moment. An electron behaves like a tiny magnet. The \(g\)-factor states how much magnetic moment a particle carries per unit of angular momentum, measured in the natural unit \(e/2m\) built from charge and mass. A classically rotating charge would have \(g = 1\). The spectral lines, however, demanded \(g = 2\) for spin, and before Dirac this value had to be inserted by hand. If the Dirac equation is coupled to a magnetic field in the simplest way compatible with relativity – an additional input that this post does not treat –, \(g = 2\) results without this value being inserted anywhere. (The calculation is not carried out here; it can be found in every textbook of relativistic quantum mechanics.)

The fine structure of hydrogen. In 1928 Charles Galton Darwin and Walter Gordon solved the Dirac equation exactly for the hydrogen atom. The energy levels that result agree exactly with a formula that Arnold Sommerfeld had already found in 1916 with a semi-classical model of the atom, and which describes the fine splitting of the spectral lines. What Sommerfeld had obtained from a fortunate combination of approximations now followed from an equation with clean foundations – including the effects that stem from spin and relativity.

It is just as honest to say where the equation ends. In 1947 Willis Lamb and Robert Retherford measured that two levels of the hydrogen atom which, according to Dirac, ought to be exactly equal lie about 1 gigahertz apart. In the same year Polykarp Kusch and Henry Foley found that the \(g\)-factor of the electron is not exactly 2 but about \(2.0023\). In 1948 Julian Schwinger explained the deviation with quantum electrodynamics, in which the electron constantly interacts with its own electromagnetic field; the first correction term gives \(g = 2\,(1 + \alpha/2\pi) \approx 2.00232\). Here \(\alpha \approx 1/137\) is the fine-structure constant, a measure of the strength of the electromagnetic interaction; it has nothing to do with the matrices \(\alpha_k\) from Section 17. Today \(g = 2.002\,319\,304\,36\ldots\) is one of the most precisely measured quantities in physics, and theory agrees with it to more than ten digits.

The Dirac equation is thus not the last word. It is, however, the foundation on which quantum electrodynamics stands. There \(\psi\) is no longer read as the state of a single electron but as a field that can create and annihilate electrons and positrons, and this field obeys the Dirac equation. Everything that came afterwards consists of corrections to it, not a replacement for it.

Section 20

Feynman’s checkerboard: mass as zigzag

Finally, the circle closes back to Act II. There it turned out that every time evolution can be calculated in two ways: by diagonalising or by summing over all paths. For the Dirac equation only the first way has been used so far – the eigenvalues \(\pm E\) are the result of a diagonalisation. In the 1940s Richard Feynman also found the second, for a world with only one direction in space, and it is so simple that one can almost play it through with a pencil on squared paper.

With only one direction of space, just two objects \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are needed that square to 1 and anticommute with each other. Two of the Pauli matrices from Section 14 already do this, and by the trace argument there \(2\times2\) is the smallest possible size; \(\psi\) then has two components. The Hamiltonian from Section 17 reads \(H = c\,\alpha\,p + \beta\,mc^2\). follows necessarily Because \(\alpha^2 = 1\), \(\alpha\) has only the eigenvalues \(+1\) and \(-1\), and if the corresponding eigenstates are chosen as the basis, \(\alpha = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1)\). Without mass, the equation for the first component then reads \(i\hbar\,\partial_t\psi_1 = c\,p\,\psi_1 = -i\hbar c\,\partial_x\psi_1\), that is \(\partial_t\psi_1 = -c\,\partial_x\psi_1\). Every function of the form \(f(x - ct)\) solves it, because differentiated with respect to \(t\) it gives \(-c f'\) and with respect to \(x\) it gives \(f'\). The shape is therefore preserved and moves to the right at the speed of light; for the second component the sign is reversed, and it moves to the left. follows necessarily And because \(\beta\) must anticommute with \(\alpha\), \(\beta\) can have no diagonal entries in this basis: for a diagonal entry, anticommutation demands \(\alpha_j\beta_{jj} = -\beta_{jj}\alpha_j\), and with \(\alpha_j = \pm1\) this means \(\beta_{jj} = 0\). Because \(\beta^2 = 1\), the product of the two off-diagonal entries is 1. convention By turning the phasor of one component suitably, both off-diagonal entries can be made equal to 1. Then \(\beta\) is the matrix that swaps the two components. The mass therefore couples the right-moving component to the left-moving one – it makes the particle change direction.

follows necessarily For a small time step \(\varepsilon\), according to Section 11, \(U \approx 1 - i\varepsilon H/\hbar\). The lattice is chosen so that a cell has the width \(c\varepsilon\); a component flying at the speed of light then moves on by exactly one cell per time step. A time step consists of two parts carried out one after the other. The part with \(\alpha\) lets the two components fly straight on, the first one cell to the right, the second one cell to the left. The part with \(\beta\) produces a corner: it turns a right-moving component into a left-moving one and vice versa, and does so with the phasor \(-i\,mc^2\varepsilon/\hbar\). That the two parts of \(H\) may be applied one after the other instead of simultaneously is because the difference is only of order \(\varepsilon^2\) and vanishes in the limit of small steps. The part with \(\beta\) alone is \(e^{-i\varphi\beta}\) with \(\varphi = mc^2\varepsilon/\hbar\). Because \(\beta^2 = 1\), \(\beta\) behaves in this exponential like a number whose square is 1, and as in Section 7 one obtains \(\cos\varphi - i\beta\sin\varphi\). Written out, this is the exactly length-preserving matrix

\[ \begin{pmatrix} \cos\varphi & -i\sin\varphi \\ -i\sin\varphi & \cos\varphi \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \varphi = \frac{mc^2\,\varepsilon}{\hbar} \]

– symbol for symbol the two-well matrix from Section 10. The two “wells” are now the two directions of flight, and the hop phasor is once again rotated by 90°, because unitarity forces it. If one multiplies out \(N\) such steps, then, according to Section 9, every term of the sum is a path: a zigzag at the speed of light whose phasor is multiplied by \(\cos\varphi \approx 1\) on every straight step and rotated by 90° and shrunk by \(\sin\varphi\) at every corner. The sum over all such zigzag paths gives, in the limit of small steps, exactly the solution of the Dirac equation. Feynman presented this construction in 1965, together with Albert Hibbs, as an exercise; that it really converges to the Dirac equation was proved by Theodore Jacobson and Lawrence Schulman in 1984.

Try it: The picture shows where the particle is to be found after each time step; it is calculated exactly with the matrix above. Start with zero mass and then increase it.

This gives mass an intuitive meaning that is not to be found in any textbook sentence about “inertial mass”. A massless particle never turns back and always flies at the speed of light. A massive particle carries, at every possible corner, the factor \(-i\sin\varphi \approx -i\,mc^2\varepsilon/\hbar\), proportional to its mass and to the duration of the step. Counted per unit of time, the amplitude for a corner is therefore \(mc^2/\hbar\), for the electron about \(7.8\cdot10^{20}\) per second. The sum over all zigzag paths makes it advance, on average, more slowly than light – like a sailing boat tacking against the wind. Mass is the amplitude for changing direction. Stated precisely, this does not mean that the electron “really” travels along one particular zigzag path; as in Act II, the individual paths are terms in a calculation, and only their sum is measurable.

It is remarkable that the same matrix \(\beta\) plays the same role in both pictures, only in a different language. In the eigenvalue picture it opens the gap between \(+mc^2\) and \(-mc^2\), from which antimatter followed in Section 18: for a particle at rest only \(\beta mc^2\) remains of \(H\), and because \(\beta^2 = 1\) its eigenvalues are \(+mc^2\) and \(-mc^2\). In the path picture it is the corner. A trace of this trembling can also be found in the full Dirac equation: in 1930 Schrödinger noticed that, according to the equation, the position of a free electron would have to oscillate back and forth with an angular frequency of about \(1.6\cdot10^{21}\,\text{s}^{-1}\) and an amplitude of about \(2\cdot10^{-13}\) metres – several hundred times less than the diameter of an atom. He called this Zitterbewegung (“trembling motion”). Its cause is the same as for the oscillation of ammonia in Section 10: a state containing parts from both energy branches has phasors rotating at \(+E/\hbar\) and \(-E/\hbar\), for a slow electron roughly \(\pm mc^2/\hbar\). Their superposition oscillates at the difference, \(2mc^2/\hbar \approx 1.6\cdot10^{21}\,\text{s}^{-1}\). It has never been observed directly in electrons; in 2010, however, a group in Innsbruck led by Rainer Blatt and Christian Roos succeeded in simulating it with a single trapped ion whose motion obeys exactly the Dirac equation.

Two limitations belong to an honest account. The checkerboard works with this simplicity only for one direction of space; for three, no equally simple version exists to this day. And the electrons in graphene, which are often cited as an example of massless Dirac particles (Novoselov, Geim and colleagues, 2005), live in a two-dimensional plane. They are relatives of the massless checkerboard, not the same thing.

Epilogue

It Is Geometry

At the beginning of this post stood a line whose symbols were unfamiliar. At the end, every single one of them can be justified, and it is worth looking back at what these justifications rest on. The post has marked every step as following necessarily, as an input or as a convention. Added up, the balance sheet reads as follows.

What was put in

  • Two requirements on a product: calculate as with numbers (brackets, multiplying out, ordinary numbers taking part as usual), and a vector times itself gives its squared length. (Section 2)
  • Five empirical facts and assumptions of quantum mechanics: probabilities can cancel; Feynman’s rule for phasors, which are complex numbers; Born’s rule; no moment in time is distinguished; energy is proportional to frequency. (Sections 7–8)
  • The foundations of relativity: light travels at the same speed for everyone, no observer is distinguished, and energy and momentum are defined via proper time. (Section 12)
  • No direction in space is singled out. (Section 16)
  • One interpretation, explicitly marked as such: Dirac’s sea, resting on Pauli’s exclusion principle. (Section 18)

What followed necessarily

The scalar product. The oriented area, and the fact that it squares negatively. That perpendicular vectors anticommute and commutativity is impossible. The imaginary unit as the unit square of the plane, and multiplication as rotation. Reflections, rotors and the half angle. The uniform rotation of stationary states, energy and momentum as rotation speeds, the translation rule. Matrix multiplication as a sum over possibilities, unitarity, the path integral, the equivalence of the path route and the eigenvalue route, the \(i\) in \(e^{-iHt/\hbar}\). The hyperbola of spacetime and Einstein’s energy formula. The conditions on Dirac’s equation, the \(\gamma\) matrices as an orthonormal basis of spacetime, their size \(4\times4\), spin ½, the four components of \(\psi\), the positive probability of finding the particle, the two energy branches with the gap \(2mc^2\), which appears as the threshold for pair creation, and mass as the amplitude for changing direction.

Some of these conclusions are only quoted here as results, because the calculation would go beyond the scope, and they are marked as such where they occur: that \(\psi\) turns with the rotor and in no other way, the probability quantity of the Klein–Gordon equation, and the convergence of the checkerboard.

What was chosen freely

The positive sense of rotation, the reference arrow \(e_1\) of the complex plane, the assignment of Hamilton’s \(i, j, k\), the right-hand rule, the sign in \(e^{-i\omega t}\), a real stay phasor, the overall sign of the metric, the sign of \(mc\) in Dirac’s equation, the concrete form of the \(\gamma\) matrices, the form of \(\beta\) in the checkerboard and the axis along which one describes spin. None of these choices changes a single prediction.

The common thread that connects all of this is rotation. In Act I it emerged that multiplication can rotate as soon as vectors are multiplied in such a way that nothing is lost, and that rotations carry a half angle within them. In Act II it emerged that quantum mechanics consists of phasors that rotate and are summed, and that matrix multiplication is exactly this summing. In Act III relativity demanded the square root of a sum of squares, and the geometry from Act I supplied it. The half angle became spin, the second sign of the square root became antimatter, and the matrix that made the square root possible in the first place became the corner in the zigzag of mass.

Since 1995 a memorial stone for Dirac has lain in the floor of Westminster Abbey, not far from Newton’s tomb. It bears no biography, only his name, his dates and the equation in its shortest form, \(i\gamma\cdot\partial\psi = m\psi\). Here \(\gamma\cdot\partial\) stands for \(\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\), and the units are chosen so that \(\hbar = c = 1\). Dirac himself said, in essence, that a physical equation must possess mathematical beauty. What this post has tried to show is one concrete reading of this beauty: the equation is the inevitable answer to a question that begins with two arrows.

Herein also lies this blog’s Glass Bead Game. The same structure – the product that generates areas and rotations – carries Hamilton’s quaternions, the attitude control of space probes, the phasors of quantum mechanics and the square root from which antimatter came. Dirac’s line is, in the end, no formula out of nowhere. It is geometry.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does the Dirac equation say?

The Dirac equation describes how the state of an electron evolves in time, and does so in a way that satisfies both quantum mechanics and the special theory of relativity. Intuitively, it states that the energy-momentum arrow of spacetime, applied to the state \(\psi\), gives the mass. From it follow the spin of the electron, its magnetic moment with \(g = 2\), the fine structure of the hydrogen spectrum and the existence of antimatter.

Why does the Dirac equation predict antimatter?

Dirac needed a square root of Einstein’s energy formula \(E^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4\), and every square root has two signs. The equation therefore has, for every momentum, solutions with positive and with negative energy. In 1930 Dirac interpreted the negative-energy states as a completely filled “sea”, and a hole in it as a particle with the mass of the electron and positive charge. Carl Anderson found this positron in 1932. Today’s quantum field theory does without the sea but confirms the result: for every particle there is an antiparticle.

Why does an electron have to turn twice to be the same again?

Rotations can be described by rotors, which contain half the angle of rotation, because a rotation through \(\theta\) arises from two reflections in mirrors with the angle \(\theta/2\) between them. For a full turn the rotor is therefore \(-1\). Vectors are multiplied by the rotor from both sides and notice nothing of this. The state of an electron, by contrast, is multiplied by the rotor from one side only and passes into its negative after \(360^\circ\), and into itself only after \(720^\circ\). Neutron interference experiments confirmed this in 1975.

What are the gamma matrices?

The four gamma matrices \(\gamma^0, \dots, \gamma^3\) are an orthonormal basis of four-dimensional spacetime, represented as \(4\times4\) matrices. They obey the same rules as the basis vectors of geometric algebra: each squares to the squared length of its direction (\(+1\) for time, \(-1\) for space), and any two different ones anticommute. Precisely for this reason their combination is a square root of the sum of squares in Einstein’s energy formula.

What is geometric algebra?

Geometric algebra is a way of calculating with vectors in which vectors can be multiplied with one another without losing information. The product of two vectors consists of the scalar product and an oriented area, the bivector. If one requires only that the product be associative and distributive and that a vector squared gives its squared length, then the complex numbers, Hamilton’s quaternions and the description of rotations in any dimension follow.

How are complex numbers, quaternions and rotations related?

The imaginary unit \(i\) can be understood as the oriented unit square of a plane: it squares to \(-1\), and multiplying by it rotates every vector of the plane by \(90^\circ\). Complex numbers are number plus area and describe rotations in the plane. In space there are three basis areas, and number plus three areas gives Hamilton’s quaternions, which describe rotations in space.

What is Feynman’s checkerboard?

Feynman’s checkerboard is a path-integral representation of the Dirac equation in one spatial dimension. A particle moves on a lattice, in every time step at the speed of light, to the left or to the right; every change of direction brings with it a factor proportional to the mass and to the duration of the time step. The sum over all zigzag paths gives the solution of the Dirac equation. Intuitively, mass is thus the amplitude for changing direction.

What is Zitterbewegung?

In 1930 Erwin Schrödinger noticed that, according to the Dirac equation, the position of a free electron would have to oscillate back and forth with an angular frequency of about \(1.6\cdot10^{21}\,\text{s}^{-1}\) and an amplitude of about \(2\cdot10^{-13}\) metres. This Zitterbewegung arises from the interference of positive- and negative-energy parts. It has never been observed directly in free electrons; in 2010 it was simulated with a trapped ion whose motion obeys the Dirac equation.
